“Now his principal doctrines were these. That atoms and the vacuum were the beginning of the universe; and that everything else existed only in opinion. ... Motion, being the cause of the production of everything, which he calls necessity. The chief good he asserts to be cheerfulness; which, however, he does not consider the same as pleasure; as some people, who have misunderstood him, have fancied that he meant; but he understands by cheerfulness, a condition according to which the soul lives calmly and steadily, being disturbed by no fear, or superstition, or other passion. He calls this state εὐθυμία, and εὐεστὼ, and several other names. Everything which is made he looks upon as depending for its existence on opinion; but atoms and the vacuum he believes exist by nature.” — Diogenes Laertius, *Lives of Eminent Philosophers*, Book IX, *Life of Democritus*, XII (trans. [Why it matters]

Only atoms, void, and necessary motion exist by nature; all macroscopic objects and sensory qualities exist merely by human convention ("opinion"), and grasping this split frees the soul from superstition into steady, fearless calm (*euthymia*). Diogenes here links Democritus' ontology directly to his ethics. The ontology draws a razor-sharp boundary between two levels of reality: what exists "by nature" (*physei* / *eteēi*)—indivisible, unchangeable atoms, the vacuum in which they move, and mechanical "necessity" (*anankē*, the vortex-motion of collisions)—and what exists "in opinion" (*nomōi*, by convention or custom), which includes every composite object and every secondary sensory quality (hot, cold, sweet, bitter, colour, and cultural valuations). The ethical payoff is *euthymia* ("cheerfulness") and *euestō* ("well-being"). Diogenes carefully distinguishes *euthymia* from crude hedonic pleasure: since the soul is a physical compound of fine, spherical atoms, "fear, or superstition, or other passion" is literally a violent jarring of the soul's atomic structure caused by mistaking conventional, ephemeral compounds ("opinion") for ultimate realities. Recognizing that only atoms and void are real stabilizes the soul's internal motion into calm equilibrium. **[INTERPRETATION: PRISCA THEOLOGIA / ESOTERIC]** In the Renaissance recovery of ancient ethics, Democritus' *euthymia* generated the famous emblem of *Democritus ridens*—the "Laughing Philosopher" paired against the weeping Heraclitus (a trope beloved by Ficino, Erasmus, and Montaigne). Ficino kept a painting of the laughing Democritus and weeping Heraclitus in his Academy at Careggi: why does Democritus laugh calmly at the world? Because in the esoteric reading of his maxim that "everything which is made depends for its existence on opinion," the entire sublunary theatre of wealth, ambition, terror, and death is exposed as a shadow-play of fleeting combinations (*opinio*), while true reality remains untouched and indestructible beneath the veil. *Euthymia* was thus read not as materialist complacency, but as the initiate's unshakeable solar serenity once he sees through the phantasmagoria of generated things.
