Card #c619IR // #c619Synthesis

A theory that only ever points toward the act you already want is permission, not reason. Raskolnikov builds an airtight doctrine, then confesses the truth of its engine: the desire came first, the argument after. 'I did the murder for myself, for myself alone.' The test is simple: strip every noble justification away, and if you would still do the deed, the theory was never the motive — it was a permit you issued yourself in advance.

— In our words · Source: Crime and Punishment (Fyodor Dostoevsky)

[Use when]

Use when you (or someone else) invoke a grand principle to justify a specific act you already want. Use when a plan's morality rests entirely on a theory of who 'deserves' or is 'entitled.' Use when deciding whether a justification is a reason or an excuse.

[Why it matters]

This is the novel's sharpest warning: moral reasoning can run backwards. The conclusion ('I may act') is fixed first, and the casuistry is built afterward to justify it — which is why the theory survives every intellectual test while remaining a lie. It names a failure mode in any person who argues brilliantly toward a conclusion they already hold.
