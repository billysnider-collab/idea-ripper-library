We cannot act in the same way with respect to the heavenly phænomena: these productions may depend upon several different causes, and we may give many different explanations on this subject, equally agreeing with the impressions of the senses. Besides, it is not here a question about reasoning on new principles, and of laying down, _à priori_, rules for the interpretation of nature; the only guides for us to follow are the appearances themselves; for that which we have in view is not a set of systems and vain opinions, but much rather a life exempt from every kind of disquietude. ... The eclipses of the sun and moon may depend either on the fact that these stars extinguish themselves, a phænomenon which we often see produced under our eyes, or on the fact of other bodies, the earth, the heaven, or something else of the same kind interposing, between them and us. ... As to those who on this point admit only one explanation as possible, they put themselves in opposition to facts, and lose sight of the bounds set to human knowledge. — Diogenes Laertius, *Lives of Eminent Philosophers*, Book X, Section XXV (Epicurus, *Letter to Pythocles*), trans. Yonge (1853). **Surface Gloss:** Writing to Pythocles, Epicurus rules that while fundamental physics has single necessary truths, distant sky-events must be explained by multiple simultaneous hypotheses drawn from everyday analogies; insisting on a single cause where we cannot get close enough to verify is vain dogmatism that breeds anxiety. **The Anti-Mythology Portfolio (Pleonachos Tropos):** When an opaque, distant event occurs (a competitor’s sudden pivot, a market crash, a senior executive's silence) where you cannot get close-range verification, the human brain defaults to *monachos tropos*: locking onto a single dramatic explanation (usually attributing godlike intent or malice to the actor) and panicking over it. Break the spell by forcing yourself to list **four distinct, mundane, mechanical explanations** drawn from everyday analogies that all fit the visible facts, and hold all four open at equal weight. The moment you refuse to crown a single story, the superstitious anxiety evaporates.

[Why it matters]

Writing to Pythocles, Epicurus rules that while fundamental physics has single necessary truths, distant sky-events must be explained by multiple simultaneous hypotheses drawn from everyday analogies; insisting on a single cause where we cannot get close enough to verify is vain dogmatism that breeds anxiety. In the *Letter to Pythocles* (Epicurus speaking in his own voice), we get the signature methodological weapon of the Garden: the **Method of Multiple Explanations (*pleonachos tropos*)**. Epicurus draws a sharp epistemic boundary between two domains: 1. **Foundational Physics and Ethics:** Here only a *single* explanation (*monachos tropos*) is valid—there are only atoms and void; pleasure is the Telos. Any alternative contradicts sensory evidence. 2. **Celestial and Meteorological Phenomena (*ta meteora*):** Eclipses, thunder, comets, lunar phases, and planetary retrogrades occur too far away for the protocol of "waiting" (*epimone*) to give us close-range verification. When investigating the distant sky, the natural philosopher must generate *several* natural explanations modeled on everyday terrestrial analogies (e.g., a lamp going out vs. a screen passing in front of it) and hold them **all as simultaneously valid** (even across different worlds in the infinite universe). Anyone who insists on a single exclusive cause (*to monachos*) for an unverified distant event violates the limits of human knowledge, slides back into theological myth-making (attributing the sky to divine micromanagement), and destroys his own mental tranquillity (*ataraxia*). **[INTERPRETATION: PRISCA THEOLOGIA / ESOTERIC]** Within the esoteric reception history, Epicurus’s ferocious attack in the *Letter to Pythocles* against the "miserable speculations of the astronomers" and those who make the stars living, divine beings regulating human fate is the exact anti-pole against which Chaldean, Stoic, and Renaissance Neoplatonic astrology defined itself. For Ficino and Pico della Mirandola, the heavens were indeed animated by rational world-souls and planetary daimones (ontological intermediaries), though Pico in his *Disputationes adversus astrologiam divinatricem* tactically deployed arguments parallel to Epicurus’s: that distant celestial bodies act only through general physical causes (light and heat), not by sitting aloft "watching the exits of these animals, and then accomplishing signs." Esoterically, Epicurus’s insistence on holding multiple sublunary analogies in suspension without collapsing them into a single dogma was read as an inverted, materialist *docta ignorantia* (learned ignorance)—a refusal to project human ego-narratives onto the abyss of the *metakosmion* (the inter-worldly spaces where the undying gods dwell in unbroken stillness).
