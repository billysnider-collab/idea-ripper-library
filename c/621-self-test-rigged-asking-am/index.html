Card #c621IR // #c621Synthesis

A doctrine that requires you to prove you are the exception disproves itself the moment you start doubting. Raskolnikov's whole crime was an experiment — 'whether I was a louse like everybody else or a man' — and the experiment's result was built into its design: 'if I asked myself whether a human being is a louse it proved that it wasn't so for me.' The very act of asking whether you are beyond the rules is the answer: the truly extraordinary do not stop to check. If you have to ask, you aren't.

— In our words · Source: Crime and Punishment (Fyodor Dostoevsky)

[Use when]

Use when you catch yourself asking whether you're 'the exception' who can skip the rules — the asking is the answer. Use when evaluating someone whose grand self-image requires constant self-reassurance. Use when distinguishing real confidence from a doctrine held together by doubt.

[Why it matters]

This is the elegant self-contradiction at the center of the novel. The Napoleon-type acts without self-interrogation; therefore self-interrogation is itself the disqualifying trait. It is a portable test for impostor-ideologies: any identity that has to be repeatedly self-asserted and self-tested is not being lived — it is being performed, and performance is the tell.
