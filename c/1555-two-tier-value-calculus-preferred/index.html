Moreover, they divide all existing things into good, bad, and indifferent... Those are indifferent which are neither beneficial nor injurious, such as life, health, pleasure, beauty, strength, riches, a good reputation, nobility of birth; and their contraries, death, disease, labour, disgrace, weakness, poverty, a bad reputation, baseness of birth, and the like... Again, of things indifferent, they call some preferred (προηγμένα), and others rejected (ἀποπροηγμένα). Those are preferred, which have some proper value (ἀξίαν), and those are rejected, which have no value at all (ἀπαξίαν ἔχοντα). And by the term proper value, they mean that quality of things, which causes them to concur in producing a well-regulated life... Again, they say that that is duty, which is preferred, and which contains in itself reasonable arguments why we should prefer it; as for instance, its corresponding to the nature of life itself; and this argument extends to plants and animals, for even their nature is subject to the obligation of certain duties. And duty (τὸ καθῆκον) had this name given to it by Zeno, in the first instance... — Diogenes Laertius, *Lives*, VII.60–62 (tr. C. D. [Why it matters]

While only virtue is good and only vice is bad, external things like health and wealth are "indifferent"—yet subdivided into "preferred" (having practical value for natural living) and "rejected," forming the basis for Zeno's concept of "duty" (*kathekon*). Here Zeno solves the fatal flaw of his Cynic teacher Crates. If virtue is the *only* good (because wealth and health can be used both well and badly, whereas true good can only benefit, just as heat can only warm), then how does a wise person make everyday choices without collapsing into Cynic paralysis or Ariston's total indifference (VII.Ariston.1)? Zeno introduces a two-tier value system. On the absolute moral tier, health, wealth, and life are strictly **indifferent** (*adiaphora*)—they cannot make your soul one millimeter more virtuous or happy. On the secondary operational tier, however, nature assigns selective **value** (*axia*) to things that support our biological and social constitution. Health and wealth are "preferred" (*proegmena*, a court metaphor: promoted close to the king, without *being* the king); sickness and poverty are "rejected" (*apoproegmena*). **Duty** (*kathekon*, "what is fitting/incumbent") is simply any action that can be given a reasonable defense (*eulogos apologia*) in selecting among these natural indifferents. **[INTERPRETATION: PRISCA THEOLOGIA / ESOTERIC]** In the esoteric reading of the Hellenistic schools, Zeno's invention of *proegmena* and *kathekon* marks the transition from the solitary, world-rejecting ascetic (the Cynic naked in his tub) to the invisible adept operating inside the machinery of empire (Zeno advising King Antigonus, later Marcus Aurelius on the Danube). The external world of matter, money, and bodily health is neither divinized (as by the vulgar) nor demonized as an evil prison (as by radical dualists); it is neutral alchemical raw material (*hyle*). Because the adept's inner peace is locked entirely to the absolute tier of Virtue, he can play the game of the secondary tier—accumulating resources, governing cities, tending the body—with total precision and zero spiritual attachment. If fortune strips away the "preferred" counters, the game-board changes, but the player's essential nature is untouched.
