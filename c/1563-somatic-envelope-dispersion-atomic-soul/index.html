Let us now return to the study of the affections, and of the sensations; for this will be the best method of proving that the soul is a bodily substance composed of slight particles, diffused over all the members of the body, and presenting a great analogy to a sort of spirit, having an admixture of heat, resembling at one time one, and at another time the other of those two principles. There exists in it a special part, endowed with an extreme mobility, in consequence of the exceeding slightness of the elements which compose it, and also in reference to its more immediate sympathy with the rest of the body. ... It is on that account that, when the soul departs, the body is no longer possessed of sensation; for it has not this power, (that of sensation namely) in itself; but, on the other hand, this power can only manifest itself in the soul through the medium of the body. ... Accordingly, they who pretend that the soul is incorporeal, utter words destitute of sense; for, if it had this character, it would not be able either to do or to suffer anything; but, as it is, we see plainly enough that it is liable to both these circumstances. — Diogenes Laertius, *Lives of Eminent Philosophers*, Book X, Section XXIV (Epicurus, *Letter to Herodotus*), trans. [Why it matters]

Epicurus argues in his *Letter to Herodotus* that the soul must be a physical body made of ultra-fine, breath-and-heat-like particles diffused through the flesh, because only a body can act or be acted upon; sensation requires the mutual containment of soul and body, and vanishes when the bodily vessel breaks. Speaking in his own voice, Epicurus demolishes the Platonic and Pythagorean doctrine of an incorporeal (*asomatos*), immortal soul. In Epicurus’s ontology, the only incorporeal thing in existence is the **vacuum (void)**, whose defining property is that it can neither act nor be acted upon—it merely provides empty room for bodies to move through. Because the soul clearly acts (moves the limbs) and suffers (feels pain and joy), it must be corporeal. It consists of exceedingly smooth, round, mobile atoms resembling wind/breath (*pneuma*) mixed with warmth, plus a "special part" of even finer, nameless atoms responsible for consciousness and sympathy. Crucially, sensation is an **emergent property of containment (*stegazon*)**: the flesh acts as a confining vessel that holds the volatile soul-atoms at a sufficient density to bounce off one another and generate feeling. When the bodily envelope ruptures at death, the soul-atoms spill out like gas into the air, instantly losing their containment and, with it, all capacity for sensation or suffering. **[INTERPRETATION: PRISCA THEOLOGIA / ESOTERIC]** To Ficino and the Renaissance Platonists, Epicurus’s argument here was the supreme materialist heresy—yet they salvaged its precise physiological mechanics by reclassifying what Epicurus was actually describing. In the Neoplatonic and Hermetic anatomy of the human composite, Epicurus had failed to see the immortal, incorporeal *Mens* (Intellect), but he had given an uncannily exact description of the **spiritus** or **astral vehicle (*ochema-pneuma*)**—the subtle, vaporous body compounded of air and celestial heat that mediates between the immaterial soul and the gross flesh. Diogenes’s parenthetical note in this very section (that Epicurus places the rational part in the chest and the irrational part diffused through the body, and that sleep occurs when soul-atoms concentrate or leak through the pores) was read by Renaissance physicians and alchemists as a literal account of the *spiritus animalis* and *spiritus vitalis*. Death, in this esoteric reading, is indeed the rupturing of the gross vessel and the dissipation of the lower pneumatic envelope, which Epicurus mistook for the extinction of the self.
