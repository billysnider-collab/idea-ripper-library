Plato says that that is an object of sensation, which is never stationary either as to its quality or its quantity, but which is always flowing and changing; as, for instance, if one take from any objects all number, then one cannot affirm that they are either equal, or of any particular things, or of what quality or quantity they are. [...] On which account he says, that ideas exist in nature as models; and that all other things are like them, and, as it were, copies of them. [...] Plato, in his theory of Ideas, says, “That since there is such a thing as memory, the ideas are in existent things, because memory is only conversant about what is stable and enduring; and that no other thing is durable except ideas, for in what way,” he continues, “could animals be preserved, if they had no ideas to guide them, and if, in addition to them, they had not an intellect given to them by nature?” But as it is they recollect similitudes, and also their food, so as to know what kind of food is fit for them; which they learn because the notion of similarity is implanted naturally in every animal; owing to which notion they recognize those of the same species as themselves. — Diogenes Laertius, *Lives of Eminent Philosophers*, Book III, Section XII, trans. [Why it matters]

Quoting the Sicilian historian Alcimus, Diogenes argues that sensible things constantly mutate, whereas eternal Ideas serve as stable models; without an innate grasp of these stable models through memory, living creatures could not even recognize food or their own species. Diogenes preserves a rare fourth-century BCE Sicilian polemic by Alcimus (who sought to prove Plato plagiarized the Dorian comic poet Epicharmus). Because Alcimus is writing very close to Plato's lifetime, his summary of the Theory of Ideas is revealingly biological rather than purely logical. Sensible matter is in perpetual quantitative and qualitative flux; strip away number, and no predicate holds. Yet biological survival requires memory (*mneme*), and memory cannot lock onto a target that has no stable identity. Therefore, the Ideas (*ideai*) operate as fixed natural paradigms (*paradeigmata*), and every living creature possesses an implanted capacity to recognize "similarity" (*homoiotes*). A hen brooding on eggs or an animal recognizing its proper diet is executing a low-level act of Platonic recollection (*anamnesis*), matching a fleeting sensory impression against an enduring internal template to stay alive. [INTERPRETATION: PRISCA THEOLOGIA / ESOTERIC] In the Hermetic and Renaissance Neoplatonic chain, Alcimus’s biological reading of the Ideas becomes the doctrine of *seminal reasons* (*rationes seminales*) and natural signatures. The Ideas are not sterile abstractions locked in a supra-celestial vault; they step down into living nature as formative seals stamped into the vital spirit of every beast and plant. When Epicharmus and Alcimus note that a dog recognizes a dog and a hen knows how to warm an egg without a human tutor, the esotericist sees *natura maga*—cosmic sympathy operating through shared archetypes. Memory is the thread linking the sublunary creature back to its celestial star-model; instinct is simply unselfconscious contemplation of the Forms.
