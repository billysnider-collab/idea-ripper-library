Now, in the Canon, Epicurus says that the criteria of truth are the senses, and the preconceptions, and the passions. But the Epicureans, in general, add also the perceptive impressions of the intellect. And he says the same thing in his Abridgment, which he addresses to Herodotus, and also in his Fundamental Principles. For, says he, the senses are devoid of reason, nor are they capable of receiving any impressions of memory. For they are not by themselves the cause of any motion, and when they have received any impression from any external cause, then they can add nothing to it, nor can they subtract anything from it. Moreover, they are out of the reach of any control; for one sensation cannot judge of another which resembles itself; for they have all an equal value. ... The Epicureans call opinion (δόξα) also supposition (ὑπόληψις). And say that it is at times true, and at times false; for that, if it is supported by testimony, and not contradicted by testimony, then it is true; but if it is not supported by testimony, and is contradicted by testimony, then it is false. On which account they have introduced the expression of “waiting,” as if, before pronouncing that a thing seen is a tower, we must wait till we come near, and learn what it looks like when we are near it. — Diogenes Laertius, *Lives of Eminent Philosophers*, Book X, Sections XX & XXII, trans. [Why it matters]

Diogenes summarizes Epicurus's *Canon*: the senses are non-rational, passive receivers that never lie because they cannot add to or subtract from external impressions; falsehood enters only through premature opinion before "waiting" for close-range sensory verification. Here Diogenes summarizes Epicurus's lost treatise *On the Criterion, or Canon*. Against Platonic rationalism and Pyrrhonian skepticism, Epicurus grounds all knowledge in three self-evident (*enargeis*) standards: 1. **Sensation (*aisthesis*):** A purely mechanical, non-rational (*alogos*) event. Because a sense organ has no memory and initiates no motion of its own, it records the physical impact of external particles exactly as they strike it. Even the visions of madmen and dreamers are "true" in the strict sense that real physical impressions are striking their minds. 2. **Preconception (*prolepsis*):** The stored mental outline formed by repeated identical sensations (e.g., the immediate general image triggered by the word "man" or "horse"), which serves as our baseline reference for inquiry and naming. 3. **Passions (*pathe*):** Pleasure and pain, the immediate biological criteria for ethics. Falsehood belongs exclusively to **supposition (*hypolepsis*)**—the mind’s interpretive leap. Hence the operational protocol of **"waiting" (*epimone*)**: suspending judgment on a distant, ambiguous impression (is the tower round or square?) until direct, close-range sensory testimony confirms or refutes the supposition. **[INTERPRETATION: PRISCA THEOLOGIA / ESOTERIC]** In the Renaissance recovery of ancient lineages, Epicurus’s insistence that "the visions of insanity and of sleep have a real object, for they act upon us" (Section XX) fascinated natural magicians and Neoplatonic readers even when they rejected his materialism. Marsilio Ficino and his circle, reading Diogenes Laertius (first translated into Latin by Ambrogio Traversari in the 1430s), re-interpreted Epicurus’s *prolepseis* ("preconceptions" or "anticipations") not as mere empirical memory-stamps, but as degraded materialist descriptions of the soul’s innate *notiones communes*—the pre-existent sparks of the Divine Mind (*Mens*) implanted before incarnation. Furthermore, the doctrine that every phantasm in dream or frenzy has an objective, active ray behind it was harmonized with the theory of astral *simulacra* and daimonic (ontological intermediary) influxes: the imagination is a passive mirror struck by subtle, real effluences descending from the celestial spheres, which only the disciplined sage can read without corrupting them through hasty mortal opinion.
