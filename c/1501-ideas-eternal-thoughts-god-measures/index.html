While matter retains the character of a Principle, (Plato) admits still other principles likewise, both the pattern-like, that is, relating to Ideas, and that of god, the father and the cause of all things. Now Idea is, as regards god, a mental operation by him; as regards us, the first thing perceptible by mind; as regards Matter, a standard; but as regards the world, perceptible by a sense, a pattern; but as considered with reference to itself, an existence... Now persons define Idea as the eternal pattern of things existing according to Nature. For it does not please the majority of Platonists (to admit) that there are Ideas of works of art, such as of a shield, or lyre; nor yet of things, that are contrary to Nature, such as of fever, and cholera; nor of what exists according to a part, as of Socrates and Plato; nor of things of no value, such as of filth and rotten thatch; nor of that, which exists with reference to something, as of a greater and superior; for Ideas are the notions of god eternal and perfect in themselves. — Alcinous, *Handbook*, Ch. 9, trans. [Why it matters]

The Ideas are the second great Principle. Viewed from five angles, an Idea is God's thought, our primary object of intellect, Matter's measure, the physical world's blueprint, and a self-subsistent essence. They exist only for natural, universal wholes—never for human artifacts, diseases, individuals, trash, or relative comparisons. This chapter contains the defining doctrinal signature of Middle Platonism: solving where the Platonic Forms "live" by housing them inside the Mind of God as his eternal "mental operations" (*noeseis*) and "notions." Alcinous maps the Idea across five relational axes: 1. **To God:** His own act of thinking. 2. **To Human Intellect:** The primary intelligible object. 3. **To Matter:** An immaterial *standard of measure* (*metron*) that bounds Matter's innate measurelessness. 4. **To the Sensible Cosmos:** The exemplary blueprint (*paradeigma*). 5. **In Itself:** Pure, self-subsistent Essence (*ousia*). Because Ideas are the eternal, self-complete thoughts of the deity, Alcinous enforces a strict fivefold exclusion clause agreed upon by "the majority of Platonists." There are no transcendent Ideas for: (a) artificial tools (shields, lyres), (b) unnatural corruptions (fevers, cholera), (c) bare individuals (Socrates, Plato), (d) worthless refuse (filth, rotten thatch), or (e) mere relations (greater, superior). Ideas govern only natural, universal species-wholes. **[INTERPRETATION: PRISCA THEOLOGIA / ESOTERIC]** When Ficino translated this chapter into Latin, he was bridging pagan Middle Platonism and the Christian Logos doctrine (originally cross-pollinated by Philo of Alexandria and Augustine, who relocated Plato’s Ideas into the Divine Wisdom). In the *prisca theologia* reading, Alcinous’s fivefold relational definition proves that ancient Platonic theology already understood the Divine Mind as an interior creative Word whose thoughts are substantial realities. Furthermore, the fivefold exclusion list becomes crucial for Renaissance natural magic and medicine: because fever, cholera, and filth have no Idea in the Mind of God, evil and disease have no substantial ontological root—they are merely failures of Matter to receive the *metron* (standard of measure) of the Idea. By aligning a sick body with the harmonic proportion of its natural species-Idea through music, herbs, and planetary talismans, the Renaissance physician restores the divine measure without having to fight a "substantial" principle of disease.
