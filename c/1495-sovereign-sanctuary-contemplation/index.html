Card #c1495IR // #c1495Synthesis

Philosophy is a longing after wisdom, or a release or withdrawal of the soul from the body, while we are turning ourselves to what is perceived by mind, and to things that exist truly... Since life is twofold, Contemplative and Active, of the former the chief point lies in the knowledge of truth; but of the latter, in doing what is suggested by reason. The Contemplative life then is the one held in honour, but the Active that which follows and is necessary... The soul then, when contemplating the deity and the thoughts of the deity, is said to be in a good state ; and this state goes by the name of intelligence ; which a person would say is nothing else than an assimilation with the deity ; and hence such would take the lead, and be held in honour, and be prayed for the most, and be the most appropriate (for man); nor is it to be hindered, and is placed in our power; and it is the cause of the end laid down for us. But Action and the Active, performed through the body, can be hindered or may be carried on, when the things, which are seen during a contemplative life, require a person to apply them to the moral conduct of man. — Alcinous, *Handbook*, Chs. 1–2, trans. [Why it matters]

Philosophy detaches the soul from the body to know true reality. Contemplation is primary, unhindered, and assimilates us to God; outward action is secondary, subject to physical obstruction, and serves to stamp contemplated truths onto human conduct. Within the Middle Platonic system, human existence is split along an ontological fault line: the intelligible realm (stable, eternal, self-identical) and the sensible realm (flowing, bodily, contingent). Because the rational soul shares the nature of the intelligible, its highest operation—Contemplation—occurs entirely inside the mind without bodily instruments. Drawing on Stoic autonomy formulas alongside classical Academic ethics, Alcinous notes that pure intellectual contemplation cannot be blocked by external tyranny or physical fortune; it is wholly in our power and constitutes our final goal: becoming like the divine through intelligence. Action, by contrast, requires the body as a tool and the external world as a medium. Therefore, civic and military duties are contingent responses to disorder. The philosopher enters politics only when poor administration forces him to stamp the patterns seen in contemplation onto human laws and youth education. **[INTERPRETATION: PRISCA THEOLOGIA / ESOTERIC]** In the Renaissance *prisca theologia* chain, Marsilio Ficino reads this passage as the foundational rule of the *vita contemplativa* and the magical-theurgical ascent of the soul. As Burges (1854) notes in his apparatus, Ficino’s manuscript read *divina* ("divine things") rather than simply "things perceptible by mind," and rendered the unhindered nature of contemplation as *"cui nulla obsistunt impedimenta, quo minus in nostra potestate consistat."* For Ficino and the Florentine Academy, "withdrawal of the soul from the body" (*solutio anime a corpore*) is not a metaphor for quiet study; it is a literal hermetic and Platonic discipline of voluntary spiritual death (*mors voluntaria*). By retracting the soul's rays from the sensible humors into the apex of the mind (*mens*), the initiate gazes directly upon the divine Ideas ("the thoughts of the deity") and becomes a living conduit capable of ordering the lower political world like a magus ordering nature.
