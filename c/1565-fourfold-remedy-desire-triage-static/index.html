And we must consider that some of the passions are natural, and some empty; and of the natural ones some are necessary, and some merely natural. And of the necessary ones some are necessary to happiness, and others, with regard to the exemption of the body, from trouble; and others with respect to living itself; for a correct theory, with regard to these things, can refer all choice and avoidance to the health of the body and the freedom from disquietude of the soul. Since this is the end of living happily; for it is for the sake of this that we do everything, wishing to avoid grief and fear; and when once this is the case, with respect to us, then the storm of the soul is, as I may say, put an end to... When, therefore, we say that pleasure is a chief good, we are not speaking of the pleasures of the debauched man, or those which lie in sensual enjoyment, as some think who are ignorant, and who do not entertain our opinions, or else interpret them perversely; but we mean the freedom of the body from pain, and of the soul from confusion. — Diogenes Laertius, *Lives of Eminent Philosophers*, Book X, Section XXVII (Epicurus, *Letter to Menoeceus*), trans. [Why it matters]

Writing to Menoeceus, Epicurus classifies desires into natural-necessary, merely natural, and empty, defining supreme pleasure not as sensual indulgence, but as the zero-state where bodily pain and mental turmoil are completely removed. In the *Letter to Menoeceus* (Epicurus speaking directly), supplemented by Diogenes’s contrast with the Cyrenaics in Section XXVIII, the entire ethical engine is laid bare: 1. **The Fourfold Remedy (*Tetrapharmakos* seeds):** (a) God is an incorruptible, blessed being who feels neither anger nor favor and causes no trouble to mortals; (b) Death is nothing to us ("when we exist, death is not present to us; and when death is present, then we have no existence"); (c) The limit of the good is easy to procure; (d) Extreme pain is brief, and chronic pain is mild. 2. **Threefold Desire Triage:** Desires are split into **Natural & Necessary** (water, plain bread, philosophy—which remove actual pain), **Natural & Non-Necessary** (costly food, sex—which merely vary pleasure without removing pain), and **Empty (*kenai*)** (wealth, statues, political crowns—born of vain opinion and insatiable). 3. **Katastematic vs. Kinetic Pleasure:** Against Aristippus and the Cyrenaics, who recognized only *kinetic* pleasure ("in motion"—the active titillation of the senses), Epicurus crowns *katastematic* pleasure ("as a condition" or state): **aponia** (zero bodily pain) and **ataraxia** (zero mental static). Once want-pain is removed by plain bread and water, pleasure has hit its absolute ceiling; luxury can only vary its flavor, never increase its height. **[INTERPRETATION: PRISCA THEOLOGIA / ESOTERIC]** In the Renaissance humanistic and esoteric revival, Epicurus’s *Letter to Menoeceus* was the primary text used by Lorenzo Valla (*De voluptate*), Erasmus, and Marsilio Ficino (who in his early youth wrote warmly of Lucretius and Epicurus before his strict Platonic turn) to rescue the "sage of the Garden" from medieval caricature as a swine of the trough. Esoterically, the closing promise to Menoeceus—"you will live like a God among men; for a man living amid immortal Gods, is in no respect like a mortal being"—was read alongside the parenthetical fragment in Maxim 1 (that the Gods exist as *eidola*—"the continual flowing on of similar images, perfected for this very purpose in human form"). By stills "the storm of the soul" through the purgation of empty desires (*kenodoxia*) and the attainment of *katastematic* equilibrium, the philosopher’s pneumatic body becomes a waveless mirror. In that state of absolute *ataraxia*, the sage no longer petitions the gods with superstitious bribes, but receives the unbroken influx of the divine *simulacra* directly from the *metakosmion*, sharing in the gods' own *autarkeia* (self-sufficiency) while still in the flesh.
