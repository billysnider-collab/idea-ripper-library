And when he made a considerable advance in philosophy he went to Alexandria, to the court of Ptolemy Philopater. And once, when there was a discussion concerning the question whether a wise man would allow himself to be guided by opinion, and when Sphærus affirmed that he would not, the king, wishing to refute him, ordered some pomegranates of wax to be set before him; and when Sphærus was deceived by them, the king shouted that he had given his assent to a false perception. But Sphærus answered very neatly, that he had not given his assent to the fact that they were pomegranates, but to the fact that it was probable that they might be pomegranates. And that a perception which could be comprehended differed from one that was only probable. — Diogenes Laertius, *Lives*, VII.Sphaerus.2 (tr. C. D. Yonge, 1853) **Surface Gloss:** When King Ptolemy tricks the Stoic Sphaerus into reaching for wax pomegranates to prove that even a sage assents to false impressions, Sphaerus replies that he assented only to the *probability* that they were pomegranates, not to the fact itself. Decouple **operational action** from **epistemic belief**. When forced to act under incomplete information, never assent to the proposition *"X is true"*; assent strictly to the proposition *"Given current evidence, X is the highest-probability bet, so I will reach for it."* If the pomegranate turns out to be wax, your action fails, but your judgment remains undefeated—because it actually *was* probable before you touched it.

— In our words · Source: Lives of Eminent Philosophers (Diogenes Laertius)

[Use when]

Making high-stakes decisions under uncertainty or active deception, where you must move decisively without marrying your ego or your worldview to an unverified assumption. ---

[Why it matters]

When King Ptolemy tricks the Stoic Sphaerus into reaching for wax pomegranates to prove that even a sage assents to false impressions, Sphaerus replies that he assented only to the *probability* that they were pomegranates, not to the fact itself. This court anecdote dramatizes the central battleground between Stoic dogmatism and Hellenistic Skepticism (compare Persaeus's twin-brother trap against Ariston in VII.Ariston.6). In Stoic psychology, an external impression (*phantasia*) is involuntary, but **assent** (*synkatathesis*)—the mind's ratification of the impression as true—is wholly voluntary. The Stoic sage never forms "opinion" (*doxa*, defined as weak or false assent). When Sphaerus reaches for the wax fruit, Ptolemy thinks he has caught the sage ratifying a non-kataleptic (false) impression. Sphaerus escapes the trap by distinguishing the propositional target of his assent: he did not assent to the axiom *"These are pomegranates"* (which under table-lighting lacks kataleptic certainty), but to the axiom *"It is reasonable/probable (*eulogon*) that these are pomegranates."* Because a rational agent must act in an uncertain environment, assenting to a probability-statement allows physical action (reaching for food) without committing cognitive error. **[INTERPRETATION: PRISCA THEOLOGIA / ESOTERIC]** Read through the lens of the Hermetic and Neoplatonic traditions, Ptolemy's banquet table is the sublunary world of *genesis*—a royal hall stocked with cunningly painted wax counterfeits designed by the demiurgic powers to trick the incarnate soul into mistaking imitations for real nourishment. The uninitiated soul bites into the wax, gives full ontological assent to the illusion, and falls under the dominion of opinion (*doxa*). The initiate (Sphaerus) moves through the exact same banquet and even extends his hand toward the world's phenomena, yet remains internally sovereign because he withholds ultimate ontological ratification. He treats the sensory world as a theatre of *euloga* (reasonable appearances), engaging the play of forms without ever surrendering the inner seal of truth to a counterfeit object.
