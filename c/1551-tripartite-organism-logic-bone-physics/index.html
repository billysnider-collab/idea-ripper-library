The Stoics divide reason according to philosophy, into three parts; and say that one part relates to natural philosophy, one to ethics, and one to logic. And Zeno, the Cittiæan, was the first who made this division, in his treatise on Reason; and he was followed in it by Chrysippus... And they compare philosophy to an animal, likening logic to the bones and sinews, natural philosophy to the fleshy parts, and ethical philosophy to the soul. Again, they compare it to an egg; calling logic the shell, and ethics the white, and natural philosophy the yolk. Also to a fertile field; in which logic is the fence which goes round it, ethics are the fruit, and natural philosophy the soil, or the fruit-trees. Again, they compare it to a city fortified by walls, and regulated by reason; and then, as some of them say, no one part is preferred to another, but they are all combined and united inseparably; and so they treat of them all in combination. — Diogenes Laertius, *Lives*, VII.33 (tr. C. D. Yonge, 1853) **Surface Gloss:** The Stoics divide philosophical reason into logic, physics, and ethics, using four organic and civic metaphors—animal, egg, fertile orchard, and walled city—to prove that the three branches form a single, inseparable living system. Audit any intellectual, creative, or organizational system using the Stoic three-layer organism test: **Wall/Bone (Logic)** = your verification rules, syntax, and error-filters; **Soil/Yolk (Physics)** = your actual model of how reality and causality work; **Fruit/Soul (Ethics)** = the behavioral output and value delivered. Cynics want fruit without walls (and get raided by sophists); pedants build walls around sterile dirt. Never teach or design one layer in isolation from the other two.

[Why it matters]

The Stoics divide philosophical reason into logic, physics, and ethics, using four organic and civic metaphors—animal, egg, fertile orchard, and walled city—to prove that the three branches form a single, inseparable living system. Within the Hellenistic school debates reported by Diogenes, this tripartite division answers both Cynic anti-intellectualism (which discarded physics and logic, as Ariston later does in VII.2) and Academic compartmentalization. Because the Stoics are strict materialist monists for whom a single rational principle (*logos*) pervades all reality, human philosophy cannot be a set of disconnected subjects; it must mirror the structure of the cosmos itself. Across all four metaphors, Logic functions as the rigid boundary and structural armature (shell, bone, perimeter wall) that prevents external sophistry from corrupting the interior. Physics (natural philosophy) is the generative substrate and nutritive medium (yolk, flesh, soil/trees) that supplies the raw causal reality of the universe. Ethics is the animating telos and harvest (soul, egg-white nourishment, fruit). To practice one without the others is to build a wall around barren dirt or harvest fruit without a fence. **[INTERPRETATION: PRISCA THEOLOGIA / ESOTERIC]** In Late Antique and Renaissance Platonic-Hermetic reception, the Stoic insistence that "no one part is preferred to another, but they are all combined and united inseparably" was read as a veiled survival of the archaic triadicinitiation. Before philosophy fractured into academic departments, the *prisci theologi* treated the three branches as stages of a single mystery: *catharsis* (logical purification of the mind's false phantasms), *epopteia* (physical contemplation of the living cosmos), and *theosis* (ethical alignment of the soul with the divine governor). The egg metaphor in particular echoed the Orphic Cosmic Egg: the outer shell of dialectic protects the alchemical marriage of cosmic nature and moral spirit inside. What looks in Diogenes like a dry curriculum debate is preserved in the esoteric tradition as the blueprint of a living microcosm, where how one reasons, how the stars move, and how one acts are one continuous operation.
