Listen and in thine heart engrave my words; Keep closed thine eye and ear 'gainst prejudice; Of others the example fear; think always for thyself (14): Consult, deliberate, and freely choose (15). Let fools act aimlessly and without cause. Thou shouldst, in the present, contemplate the future (16). — *The Golden Verses of Pythagoras*, Purification, trans. Nayan Louise Redfield (1917), p. 116. **Surface Gloss:** Seal your mind against social pressure and herd mimicry. Deliberate independently before every action so you never act with future regret, leaving aimless reactivity to fools. **The Future-Regret Filter:** Before committing to any speech or action, run two checks: (1) *Mimicry Audit*—am I doing this only because someone else's words or example pressured me? (2) *Retrospective Simulation*—if I stand in the future looking back at this exact present moment, will this action cause me pain? If yes, abort.

— In our words · Source: The Golden Verses of Pythagoras

[Use when]

Resisting FOMO (fear of missing out), peer pressure, persuasive sales pitches, or high-velocity environments pushing you to act before you have thought. ---

[Why it matters]

Seal your mind against social pressure and herd mimicry. Deliberate independently before every action so you never act with future regret, leaving aimless reactivity to fools. This passage (Greek lines 24–26, p. 116) is the core cognitive firewall of the *Golden Verses*: "Let no one by word or by deed persuade you to do or say whatever is not best for you (*beltion*)." Social contagion—acting by reflex because someone else spoke persuasively or set a flashy example—is treated as a primary vector of moral ruin. The initiate must "consult and deliberate before the act" (*bouleuou de pro ergou*), lest foolish outcomes (*mōra*) ensue. The operational test of an action's validity is **temporal projection**: only execute actions "which will not grieve/pain you afterward" (*ha se mē metepeit' aniēsēi*—rendered by d'Olivet/Redfield as "in the present, contemplate the future"). A "fool" (*deilos anēr*) is defined mechanically as an agent who acts without calculating second-order future consequences. [INTERPRETATION: PRISCA THEOLOGIA / ESOTERIC] In the Renaissance Platonic reading (especially Pico della Mirandola's *Oration on the Dignity of Man*, which draws heavily on Pythagorean and Chaldean sources), this section defines the sovereign freedom of the human microcosm. Unlike beasts (bound by instinct) or angels (fixed in eternity), the human soul stands at the crossroads of time. To "think always for thyself" and "freely choose" is to exercise the divine prerogative of *liberum arbitrium* (free will). When the initiate closes his eyes and ears to the siren song of the multitude ("the example of others"), he withdraws from the realm of *doxa* (opinion/phantasy) into *noēsis* (intellectual vision), seeing future effects coiled inside present causes just as Providence does.
