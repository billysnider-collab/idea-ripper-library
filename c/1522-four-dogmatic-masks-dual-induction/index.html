Plato then explains the opinions which he entertains himself, and refutes false ones; and about doubtful matters he suspends his judgment. His opinions of matters as they appear to him he puts into the mouth of four persons, Socrates, Timæus, an Athenian poet, and an Eleatic stranger. But the strangers are not, as some people have supposed, Plato and Parmenides, but certain nameless imaginary characters. Since Plato asserts as undeniable axioms all the opinions which he puts into the mouth of Socrates or Timæus. But when he is refuting false propositions, he introduces such characters as Thrasymachus, and Callicles, and Polus, and Gorgias, and Protagoras, Hippias too, and Euthydemus, and men of that stamp. But when he is demonstrating anything, then he chiefly uses the inductive form of argument, and that too not of one kind only, but of two. For induction is an argument, which by means of some admitted truths establishes naturally other truths which resemble them. But there are two kinds of induction; the one proceeding from contraries, the other from consequents. — Diogenes Laertius, *Lives of Eminent Philosophers*, Book III, Section XXXIII, trans. C. D. [Why it matters]

Against skeptics who claim Plato never states positive doctrines, Diogenes explains that Plato speaks his own views through four specific dramatic characters, exposes falsehood through named sophists, and uses two distinct types of induction—one to trap opponents, another to build his own proofs. [Note: Yonge translates *Athenaios xenos* ("Athenian stranger") as "an Athenian poet," misreading or emending *xenos*.] Diogenes draws on Middle Platonic isagogic (introductory) handbooks designed to settle the fierce Hellenistic debate between the Skeptical New Academy (which claimed Plato suspended judgment on everything) and the Dogmatic Platonists. The compiler resolves the dispute by separating Plato's dramatic cast into functional operators: four positive mouthpieces (Socrates, Timaeus, the Athenian Stranger, the Eleatic Stranger) carry affirmative doctrine (*dogmata*), while historical sophists (Thrasymachus, Callicles, Gorgias) serve as refutative targets. This dramatic split is paired with a logical split in induction (*epagoge*): 1. **Induction from Contraries (Eristic/Refutative):** Locking an opponent into a binary dilemma where either answer ("same" or "different") forces a self-contradiction. Used strictly to demolish false conceit, never to build doctrine. 2. **Induction from Consequents (Dialectic):** Moving from admitted particular observations (waking follows sleeping) to a universal law (contraries generate contraries) to establish positive metaphysical truth. [INTERPRETATION: PRISCA THEOLOGIA / ESOTERIC] In the esoteric reading of the Platonic corpus, Plato's refusal to speak in his own first-person voice (*in propria persona*) is the supreme signature of the initiate teacher. Truth cannot be handed over as a flat treatise without being profaned by unprepared readers; it must be refracted through four archetypal masks corresponding to the four levels of wisdom: Socrates (ethical purification and love), Timaeus (Pythagorean cosmic generation), the Eleatic Stranger (ontological division of Being), and the Athenian Stranger (sacred legislation). The refutative dilemmas are the outer courtyard's cathartic fire, burning away sophistic illusions through binary paradox before the soul is permitted to ascend the ladder of consequent induction toward the immortal.
