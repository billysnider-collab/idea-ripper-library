That which thou dost not know, pretend not that thou dost. Instruct thyself: for time and patience favour all (17). — *The Golden Verses of Pythagoras*, Purification, trans. Nayan Louise Redfield (1917), p. 118. **Surface Gloss:** Never attempt or fake competence in things you have not learned; instead, submit to systematic instruction over time, which unlocks the most pleasant life. **The Competence Boundary Rule:** Draw a hard line between your circle of actual mastery and your circle of ignorance. Never fake execution outside that line to save face; instead, switch immediately into apprentice mode ("Instruct thyself"). Mastery compounded quietly over time beats performative competence every single time.

[Why it matters]

Never attempt or fake competence in things you have not learned; instead, submit to systematic instruction over time, which unlocks the most pleasant life. The facing Greek (p. 118) is blunt and operational: *Prēsse de mēden tōn mē epistasi, alla didaskeu hossa chreōn*—"Do nothing of the things you do not understand (*epistasi*), but be taught (*didaskeu*) whatever is necessary, and thus you will live a most delightful life." In the Pythagorean guild, knowledge (*epistēmē*) is structural and cumulative, modeled on geometry, music, and medicine. Pretending to know what one does not know introduces immediate discord into action. True competence requires submission to *didaxis* (structured apprenticeship) across time (*chronos*). Historically, Pythagorean novices underwent a five-year period of silent listening (*echemythia*) before being permitted to speak or operate independently. By eliminating the impostor's anxiety and only executing mastered protocols, the practitioner achieves *terpnotaton bion*—a life of frictionless joy. [INTERPRETATION: PRISCA THEOLOGIA / ESOTERIC] In the initiatory reception of the *prisca theologia*, this rule governs the transition from the profane world into the lesser mysteries. Amateurism in sacred science—attempting theurgic or harmonic operations without having undergone the long, patient purification of the mathematical quadrivium—was regarded by Iamblichus and his Renaissance heirs as spiritually fatal. "Time and patience" (*la constance, au temps* in d'Olivet's French, p. 119) refer to the slow, Saturnian maturation required for the soul's intellectual eye to adjust to divine light. Only those who confess their ignorance and endure the multi-year silence of the vestibule are granted the keys to the sanctuary.
