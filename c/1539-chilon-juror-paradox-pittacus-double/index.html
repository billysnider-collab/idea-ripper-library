They say, too, that when he [Chilo] was old he said, that he was not conscious of having ever done an unjust action in his life; but that he doubted about one thing. For that once when judging in a friend’s cause he had voted himself in accordance with the law, but had persuaded a friend to vote for his acquittal, in order that so he might maintain the law, and yet save his friend." "He [Pittacus] was also a lawgiver; and he made a law that if a man committed a crime while drunk, he should have double punishment; in the hope of deterring men from getting drunk, as wine was very plentiful in the island... Another of his sayings was, 'Even the Gods cannot strive against necessity.' Another was, 'Power shows the man.' — Diogenes Laertius, *Lives*, Book I, Chilon III & Pittacus III–IV (trans. C. D. Yonge, 1853) **Surface Gloss:** Chilon confesses a single lifelong moral doubt: voting strictly by the law as a juror while persuading a fellow juror to acquit his friend. Pittacus enacts a law doubling the penalty for crimes committed while drunk, and notes that power reveals character. **The Pittacus Multiplier (No Excuse from Self-Induced Blindness):** When someone causes damage while under the influence of a state they voluntarily entered—whether alcohol, hype, sleep-deprivation, or a "move fast and break things" frenzy—do not treat their impaired state as a mitigating factor. Double the accountability: once for the damage done, and once for deliberately disabling their own brakes beforehand.

[Why it matters]

Chilon confesses a single lifelong moral doubt: voting strictly by the law as a juror while persuading a fellow juror to acquit his friend. Pittacus enacts a law doubling the penalty for crimes committed while drunk, and notes that power reveals character. Pairing the Spartan ephor Chilon with the Mytilenean *aisymnetes* (elected dictator) Pittacus exposes the friction between personal loyalty (*philia*), passion, and impersonal statute (*nomos*) in archaic Greek ethics. Chilon's deathbed confession isolates the classic conflict of the magistrate: how to satisfy the invariant rule of law and the particular obligation to a friend simultaneously. His split-ballot maneuver—casting his own vote for conviction to keep his judicial oath clean, while lobbying a colleague to cast the acquitting vote—leaves him in lifelong aporia because he preserved the *letter* of his ballot while subverting the *aggregate outcome* of the court. Pittacus attacks a different legal loophole: the plea of diminished capacity. Because Lesbos produced abundant wine, Pittacus refuses to treat intoxication as a mitigating excuse; instead, he penalizes the *voluntary choice to lower one's self-governance* whenever it results in harm, doubling the sentence to force pre-commitment to sobriety. **[INTERPRETATION: PRISCA THEOLOGIA / ESOTERIC]** In the Pythagorean and Neoplatonic reading of the Sages, Pittacus's "double punishment for the drunken offender" is a cosmic law of the soul's descent. Wine is the traditional symbol of *genesis* and material forgetfulness (the Cup of Dionysus into which the descending soul drinks and forgets the intelligible world, as developed in Macrobius and Ficino). The soul that sins through ignorance after willingly intoxicating itself with sensual matter incurs a double karmic burden: one for the act itself, and a second for voluntarily extinguishing the watchman of *Nous*. Likewise, Pittacus's maxim that "even the Gods cannot strive against necessity (*ananke*)" reflects the subordination of the cosmic gods to the invariant geometric laws of the Demiurge, while Chilon's "Keep a watch upon one's self" (Chilon II) is the Spartan seed of the contemplative *prosoche* (inner vigilance).
