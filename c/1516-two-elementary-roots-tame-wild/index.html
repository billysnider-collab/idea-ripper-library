Now an Affection is an irrational movement of the soul, as regards either an ill or a good. And a movement has been called irrational, because Affections are neither decisions nor opinions, but movements of the irrational portions of the soul. For in the part of the soul, subject to Affections, there exist things, which, although they are our works, are nevertheless not in our power. ... For there are two Affections, simple and elementary, (namely,) Pleasure and Pain, and from these the rest are formed. For we must not number with these Fear and Desire, as being of the nature of principles and simple. ... But of Affections some are of a wild kind, others of a tame. Now the tame are such as exist in man according to nature; (being) both necessary and proper; and they are in this state, while they preserve some measure; but when there is found in them a want of measure, they then become deviations from right. Of such a kind are Pleasure, Pain, Anger, Pity, Shame. ... But other Affections, which are contrary to Nature, are of a wild kind, and arise from a perversion (of mind), and improper habits. Of such a kind is (excessive) laughter, and a rejoicing over calamities, and a hatred of mankind; which, by being stretched out and relaxed, and existing in any state whatsoever, are deviations from right, through not receiving any moderation. — Alcinous, *Handbook of Platonism*, Ch. 31, trans. George Burges (1854), pp. 306–308. *Surface Gloss:* Affections are non-rational soul movements—"our works, yet not in our power." Against the Stoics, there are only two simple root affections (Pleasure and Pain), while Desire and Fear are mixed compounds. Affections split into "tame" (natural and useful when moderated) and "wild" (perverse at any intensity). **The Two-Element Emotional Chemistry & The Tame/Wild Split:** 1. Every complex human emotion (ambition, dread, envy, nostalgia) is just a secondary compound mixed from two primary elements: Pleasure and Pain. Even Fear has a drop of hopeful pleasure in it (once hope hits zero, fear turns into flat grief); even Desire is heavy with the pain of not-having-yet. 2. Separate emotions into **Tame** (anger, pity, shame—functional hardware as long as you keep them dialed to a moderate setting) and **Wild** (Schadenfreude, cynicism/misanthropy, derisive mockery—toxic at *any* dosage, with no "healthy middle ground").

— In our words · Source: Handbook of Platonism (Alcinous)

[Use when]

Breaking down complex character motivations into mixes of hope and dread, or deciding which organizational behaviors to calibrate/moderate ("tame") versus which to zero-tolerance purge ("wild"). ---

[Why it matters]

Alcinous dismantles the Stoic taxonomy of the passions on three fronts: 1. **Ontology of the Passion:** Against Chrysippus, who defined affections (*pathe*) as voluntary rational judgments (*kriseis*) or opinions (*doxai*), Alcinous defines them as involuntary motions of the non-rational soul—paradoxically "our works, yet not in our power" (*hemetera erga, ouk eph' hemin*), proven by the fact that we feel fear or arousal even after our rational mind has judged an object harmless or worthless. 2. **Reduction of the Four Roots to Two:** The Stoics taught four primary, simple passions: Pleasure (present good), Pain (present evil), Desire (future good), and Fear (future evil). Drawing on Plato's *Philebus* and *Laws* (644c), Alcinous reduces the elementary roots (*archoeide kai hapla*) strictly to **Pleasure and Pain**. Desire and Fear are *compounds*, not elements: Desire contains the pleasure of hopeful expectation mixed with the pain of uncertainty; Fear contains dominant pain mixed with a lingering thread of hope for escape (since total despair ceases to fear and simply grieves). 3. **Tame (*hemera*) vs. Wild (*agria*):** Against the Stoic demand for total *apatheia* (eradication of all passion), Alcinous defends Aristotelian *metriopatheia* for **tame** affections (anger for defense, pity for humanity, shame to flee baseness), while isolating **wild** affections (mocking laughter, *epichairekakia*/Schadenfreude, misanthropy) which have no virtuous mean and are corrupt at *any* dose. [INTERPRETATION: PRISCA THEOLOGIA / ESOTERIC] Burges (1854) notes Ficino's lucid rendering of the fear-paradox: *si malorum repulsionem vel levationem penitus desperarit, haud ultra metueret; immo acriori inde passione afflictus doleret jam potius quam timeret* ("if one utterly despaired of repelling or lightening evils, he would fear no further, but rather grieve than fear"). In the Hermetic and Renaissance moral cabala, the reduction of all complex emotional weather (jealousy, regret, ambition, terror) to a binary alchemical mixture of two primal salts—Pleasure (expansion toward perceived good) and Pain (contraction from perceived evil)—provided a master key to soul-craft. Furthermore, the distinction between *tame* and *wild* passions resolved the war between Stoic ascetics and Peripatetic humanists: the initiate does not castrate the natural animal of the soul (anger, pity, shame are divine tools planted by Providence), but domesticates the tame beasts with the bridle of the Mean, while ruthlessly exterminating the "wild" parasites (Schadenfreude, misanthropy) that grow only out of a twisted habitus.
