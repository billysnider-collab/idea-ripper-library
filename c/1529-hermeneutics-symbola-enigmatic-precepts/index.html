XVI. For he was very eminent for his power of attracting friendships; and among other things, if ever he heard that any one had any community of symbols with him, he at once made him a companion and a friend. XVII. Now, what he called his symbols were such as these. “Do not stir the fire with a sword.” “Do not sit down on a bushel.” “Do not devour your heart.” “Do not aid men in discarding a burden, but in increasing one.” “Always have your bed packed up.” “Do not bear the image of a God on a ring.” “Efface the traces of a pot in the ashes.” “Do not wipe a seat with a lamp.” [...] “When you are travelling abroad, look not back at your own borders.” Now the precept not to stir fire with a sword meant, not to provoke the anger or swelling pride of powerful men; not to violate the beam of the balance meant, not to transgress fairness and justice; not to sit on a bushel is to have an equal care for the present and for the future, for by the bushel is meant one’s daily food. By not devouring one’s heart, he intended to show that we ought not to waste away our souls with grief and sorrow. In the precept that a man when travelling abroad should not turn his eyes back, he recommended those who were departing from life not to be desirous to live, and not to be too much attracted by the pleasures here on earth. — Diogenes Laertius, *Lives of Eminent Philosophers*, Book VIII, Sections XVI–XVII, trans. [Why it matters]

Pythagoras used enigmatic ritual maxims called *symbola* as recognition tokens among initiates; beneath their bizarre literal surface (not poking fire with a sword, not sitting on a grain-measure) lay coded ethical and thanatological rules. In Greek antiquity, a *symbolon* was originally half of a broken knucklebone or tally-stick kept by two guest-friends; when fitted together, the jagged edges proved identity. Diogenes (drawing on Aristotle's lost *On the Pythagoreans* and Androcydes) shows how the Pythagorean brotherhood weaponized this mechanism linguistically. If a stranger shared "community of symbols" (*symbolon koinonian*), he was instantly recognized as an initiate. Structurally, the *symbola* (or *akousmata*) operate on a two-tier semantic lock: - **Tier 1 (Archaic Ritual Taboo):** Literal cultic regulations governing hearth-fire, ash-prints, grain measures (*choinix*), and threshold crossings, akin to Hesiodic or Orphic purity rules. - **Tier 2 (Allegorical Ethical Decode):** A portable moral and psychological operating system invented when the brotherhood dispersed. "Efface the pot's trace in the ashes" means leave no lingering resentment after anger cools; "pack up your bed" means maintain constant readiness; "look not back at your borders when travelling" governs the soul's clean detachment at the hour of physical death. [INTERPRETATION: PRISCA THEOLOGIA / ESOTERIC] For Marsilio Ficino, Lilio Gregorio Giraldi, and Erasmus, the Pythagorean *symbola* were the direct bridge between Egyptian hieroglyphs (which Pythagoras studied in the temples, VIII.3) and Greek philosophy. A *symbolon* protects sacred truth by wrapping it in an absurd or mundane rind: the profane reader laughs at "do not wipe a seat with a lamp" or "abstain from beans" (VIII.19) and walks past, while the initiate cracks the shell. "Do not wear the image of God on a ring" commands the initiate not to stamp divine metaphysics onto external, material tokens for public display; "efface the pot-mark in the ashes" means that once the mind ascends to pure *nous*, it must wipe away the phantasms and corporeal impressions left in the lower imagination; and "looking not back at the borders" is the Orphic-Pythagorean instruction against the backward glance of Orpheus that drags the departing soul back into generation.
