Card #c1517IR // #c1517Synthesis

For the bad is the love of the body alone, through its being overcome by what is pleasant; and this is after the manner of beasts; but the well-behaved is for the sake of the naked soul, in which there is seen a fitness for virtue; but the intermediate has a longing for the body, and a longing likewise for the beauty of the soul. He too, who is worthy to be loved, is himself a mean, as being neither ill-conducted nor well-behaved; from whence we must call the love, that lays claim to the body, some demon rather than a god, who has never been generated in an earthly body, (and) is the conveyer of what is sent by the gods to man, and conversely. The amatory then, being thus commonly divided into the three kinds before mentioned, the one, which relates to the love of the good, being freed from an affection, becomes a thing of art; from whence it is placed in the rational (portion) of the soul; and its contemplations are to know the person worthy to be loved, and to possess and make use of him; and further to judge of him from his propensities and impulses, whether they are noble, and tending to what is honourable, and whether they are violent and fervid. And he, who strives to possess it, shall possess it, not by rendering delicate or praising the object of his love, but by repressing it rather, and showing that by a person, being in the state he is now, life is not to be lived. And when he gets the party loved into his power, he will make use of him, after having enjoined the things, through which he will, after being practised in them, become perfect; and the end to them (will be), that, instead of a lover and a beloved, they will become friends. — Alcinous, *Handbook of Platonism*, Ch. 32, trans. George Burges (1854), pp. 310–311. *Surface Gloss:* Love has three grades: beastly (for body alone), intermediate (for body and soul), and noble (for the naked soul's virtue). Because it mediates between flesh and spirit, Love is a *daimon*—an unembodied courier between gods and men. Noble love becomes a rational art that wins the beloved not by flattery, but by humbling him into perfection, turning asymmetrical passion into equal friendship. **The Anti-Flattery Capture (Socratic Mentorship as an Art):** Cheap suitors, bad managers, and sycophants try to win high-potential talent by praising and spoiling them. The master-practitioner treats mentorship as a three-step rational art: 1. **Screen for Voltage:** Look for raw impulses that are noble, intense, and "fervid." 2. **Capture by Humbling:** Win them not with flattery, but by dismantling their vanity and showing them that their current level of existence is unlivable compared to what they could be. 3. **Graduate to Equals:** Put them through the regimen until the asymmetrical mentor-protege dynamic dissolves into peer friendship.

— In our words · Source: Handbook of Platonism (Alcinous)

[Use when]

Recruiting and forging high-potential proteges who are immune to cheap praise, or writing a mentor-student relationship that avoids sentimental cliches. ---

[Why it matters]

Synthesizing Plato's *Symposium* (202d–203a), *Phaedrus*, *Lysis*, and *Alcibiades I*, Alcinous maps the mechanics of friendship (*philia*) and erotic love (*erotike*). True friendship is strict reciprocal goodwill (*antiphilia*) between moderate equals ("like is friendly to like, when they are in moderation"; immoderate people cannot harmonize even with each other). Erotic love, however, begins in asymmetry and splits into three species matching the three moral states of the soul: 1. **Base (*phaule*):** beastly appetite for the body alone. 2. **Intermediate (*mese*):** mixed desire for bodily beauty and psychic beauty. 3. **Urban/Noble (*asteia*):** love for the "naked soul" (*psiles tes psyches*) containing an aptitude for virtue. Crucially, both the worthy beloved (*axierastos*) and embodied Eros itself occupy an ontological **Mean**: Eros is a *daimon* (an intermediary spiritual being, never incarnated in an earthly body, who shuttles divine transmissions to humans and human prayers to gods). When purified of passive affection (*pathos*), noble love becomes a rational **Science/Art** (*techne*) seated in the *logistikon* with three operational theorems: (a) **Diagnose** the worthy beloved by testing whether his impulses are noble, intense, and fervid (*sphodrai te kai diapyroi*); (b) **Capture** him not by spoiling or flattering him (*ouk thrypton oude epainon*), which is the vulgar lover's move, but by *repressing/humbling* him (*kolazon*) and proving that his current unexamined state makes life not worth living (Socrates' exact move on Alcibiades); and (c) **Deploy** him through disciplined practice until the asymmetrical hierarchy of Lover/Beloved dissolves into the equal symmetry of Friends. [INTERPRETATION: PRISCA THEOLOGIA / ESOTERIC] In the history of Western esotericism, this chapter is one of the direct structural blueprints for Marsilio Ficino's *De Amore* (1469), the foundational manifesto of Renaissance *amor platonicus*. Burges (1854) preserves Ficino's expanded Latin rendering of the Socratic capture: *nec assentabitur illi, neque suavibus verbis obsequiisque deleniet, sed absterrebit potius, atque docebit nequaquam illi, donec talis sit, esse vivendum* ("he will neither flatter him nor soothe him with sweet words and obsequiousness, but rather deter him, and teach him that he must by no means live so long as he is such"). In Ficino's reception, Eros is explicitly a cosmic *daimon* (strictly an ontological mediator in the pagan hierarchy, never a Christian devil) who binds the higher and lower worlds. The esoteric master practices *ars amatoria* as a spiritual alchemy: he spots a soul with "fervid and fiery" raw voltage, shatters the youth's narcissistic ego through ruthless Socratic elenchus, and then guides him up the ladder until the asymmetrical voltage of master-disciple resolves into the golden equilibrium of initiate brotherhood (*ut ex amore amicitia fiat*).
