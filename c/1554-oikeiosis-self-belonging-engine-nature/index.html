They say that the first inclination which an animal has is to protect itself, as nature brings herself to take an interest in it from the beginning, as Chrysippus affirms in the first book of his treatise on Ends; where he says, that the first and dearest object to every animal is its own existence, and its consciousness of that existence. For that it is not natural for any animal to be alienated from itself, or even to be brought into such a state as to be indifferent to itself, being neither alienated from nor interested in itself. It remains, therefore, that we must assert that nature has bound the animal to itself by the greatest unanimity and affection; for by that means it repels all that is injurious, and attracts all that is akin to it and desirable. But as for what some people say, that the first inclination of animals is to pleasure, they say what is false. For they say that pleasure, if there be any such thing at all, is an accessory only, which, nature, having sought it out by itself, as well as those things which are adapted to its constitution, receives incidentally in the same manner as animals are pleased, and plants made to flourish. — Diogenes Laertius, *Lives*, VII.52 (tr. C. D. Yonge, 1853) **Surface Gloss:** Against the Epicureans, the Stoics argue that a creature's primary drive is not pleasure, but self-preservation and self-awareness (*oikeiosis*); pleasure is merely an incidental byproduct that appears when an organism thrives according to its constitution. Never optimize a system, career, or life for **pleasure/metrics (the bloom)**; optimize for **constitutional integrity (the root)**. Ask of any habit, tool, or environment: *"Does this belong to my constitution and strengthen my core agency, or does it alienate me from myself?"* Treat happiness, applause, and pleasure strictly as dashboard indicator lights that blink on incidentally when an organism is doing what it was built to do.

[Why it matters]

Against the Epicureans, the Stoics argue that a creature's primary drive is not pleasure, but self-preservation and self-awareness (*oikeiosis*); pleasure is merely an incidental byproduct that appears when an organism thrives according to its constitution. This passage is the foundation of Stoic ethics and its primary weapon against Epicurus (who argued in Book X that the cradle-behavior of infants proves pleasure is the chief good). Chrysippus counters with the doctrine of *oikeiosis*—nature's act of making a creature "belong to itself" through immediate proprioceptive consciousness (*syneidesis*) of its own constitution. Before an infant can calculate pleasure or pain, it feels an innate affinity for its own structural integrity, repelling what damages its constitution and appropriating what preserves it. Pleasure (*epigennema*, Yonge's "accessory") is demoted from the goal of action to a secondary symptom—like the bloom on a healthy plant or the friskiness of a well-fed animal. As the human animal matures and reason (*logos*) supervenes as the craftsman of impulse (VII.52), *oikeiosis* shifts its target: our true constitution is no longer the animal body, but rational nature itself, culminating in alignment with cosmic law (VII.53). **[INTERPRETATION: PRISCA THEOLOGIA / ESOTERIC]** In the Renaissance synthesis of Ficino and Pico, the Stoic doctrine of *oikeiosis*—and its climax in VII.53, where happiness is defined as "harmony with the genius [*daimon*] of each individual with reference to the will of the universal governor"—was recognized as the ladder of cosmic sympathy. Nature implants a spark of self-love in every creature not to trap it in biological egoism, but as a homing beacon. The soul begins by loving its own bodily vessel, then recognizes its true *oikeion* (what is akin to it) in the rational *daimon* within, and finally realizes that its individual *daimon* is consubstantial with Jupiter, the World-Soul. Pleasure is merely the friction-heat or floral scent given off when a being clicks into alignment with its archetype; to chase the scent instead of the alignment is the primordial fall into sensuality.
