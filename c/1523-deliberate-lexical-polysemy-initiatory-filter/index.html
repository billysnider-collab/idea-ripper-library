XXXVIII. But he employs a great variety of terms in order to render his philosophical system unintelligible to the ignorant. In his phraseology he considers wisdom as the knowledge of things which can be understood by the intellect, and which have a real existence: which has the Gods for its object, and the soul as unconnected with the body. [...] He also uses the same words in different senses at different times. Accordingly he uses φαῦλος in the sense of ἁπλοῦς, _simple_, [...] But Plato uses the word sometimes even for what is beautiful; and sometimes for small and insignificant; and very often he uses different words to express the same idea. Accordingly, besides the word ἰδέα for a class, he uses also εἶδος, and γένος, and παράδειγμα, and ἀρχὴ, and αἴτιον. Sometimes he uses opposite expressions for the same thing; accordingly, he says that it is an object of sensation that exists, while at other times he says it is that which does not exist; speaking of it as existing because of its origin, and as non-existent with reference to its continual changes. — Diogenes Laertius, *Lives of Eminent Philosophers*, Book III, Section XXXVIII, trans. [Why it matters]

Plato intentionally varies his vocabulary—using one word for many meanings, many words for one concept, and even contradictory predicates for the same object—so that uninitiated, literal-minded readers cannot extract his system mechanically. To a Peripatetic or Stoic logician who demands one-to-one correspondence between technical terms and concepts, Plato's fluid prose looks sloppy. Diogenes’ Middle Platonic source flips this critique on its head: Plato's terminological fluidity (*polyonymia* and *homonymia*) is a deliberate pedagogical defense mechanism (*pragmateia*) to keep the uninstructed (*amathoi*) from memorizing a jargon checklist and mistaking the words for the realities. By rotating six different terms (*idea, eidos, genos, paradeigma, arche, aition*) for the single reality of the Form, and applying contradictory predicates ("existent" vs. "non-existent") to sensible matter depending on the angle of inquiry (its causal origin vs. its perpetual mutation), Plato forces the reader to look past the lexical token to the underlying noetic structure. If you only read at the level of fixed dictionary definitions, the text self-locks. [INTERPRETATION: PRISCA THEOLOGIA / ESOTERIC] Within the esoteric tradition, this passage is the explicit charter for Platonic *occultatio* (hiding in plain sight). Where Pythagoras hid his teachings behind a five-year acoustic curtain and enigmatic *symbola*, Plato hid his mysteries inside the dazzling surface of literary Attic Greek. By rotating names for the divine archetypes and speaking in apparent contradictions ("neither moving nor stationary," "one and many"), Plato prevents the profane from seizing the sacred doctrines with unwashed discursive habits. Furthermore, shifting terminology trains the mind's eye to detach from rigid linguistic idols, preparing the intellect for the supra-rational intuition where opposite predicates coincide in the One.
