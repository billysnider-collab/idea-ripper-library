And all perturbation is itself, according to Zeno, a movement of the mind, or superfluous inclination, which is irrational, and contrary to nature. Moreover, of the superior class of perturbations, as Hecaton says, in the second book of his treatise on the Passions, and as Zeno also says in his work on the Passions, there are four kinds, grief, fear, desire, and pleasure. And they consider that these perturbations are judgments, as Chrysippus contends in his work on the Passions; for covetousness is an opinion that money is a beautiful object, and in like manner drunkenness and intemperance, and other things of the sort, are judgments. And grief they define to be an irrational contraction of the mind... But fear is the expectation of evil... Again, desire is an irrational appetite... Again, pleasure is an irrational elation of the mind over something which appears to be desirable... There are also three good dispositions of the mind; joy, caution, and will. And joy they say is the opposite of pleasure, since it is a rational elation of the mind; so caution is the opposite of fear, being a rational avoidance of anything, for the wise man will never be afraid, but he will act with caution; and will, they define as the opposite of desire, since it is a rational wish. — Diogenes Laertius, *Lives*, VII.63 (tr. [Why it matters]

The Stoics define the four root passions (grief, fear, desire, pleasure) not as animal instincts, but as false value-judgments; the sage replaces them with three rational dispositions (*eupatheiai*): joy, caution, and will—leaving no rational counterpart for grief. Against Plato and Aristotle, who divided the soul into a rational charioteer and irrational horses that must be tamed, Chrysippus enforces psychological monism: the ruling center (*hegemonikon*) is wholly rational, and passions (*pathe*, Yonge's "perturbations") are **corrupted judgments** (*kriseis*). A passion occurs when reason makes a two-fold cognitive error: it judges an indifferent external thing to be an absolute good or evil, and it judges that a violent pneumatic reaction (contraction in grief, swelling/elation in pleasure, stretching in desire, shrinking in fear) is appropriate. These reactions are mapped on a 2x2 grid of **Apparent Good vs. Apparent Evil** across **Present vs. Future**. Crucially, the Stoic sage is not a stone; he possesses the three *eupatheiai* ("good dispositions"): irrational Pleasure becomes rational **Joy** (*chara*); irrational Fear becomes rational **Caution** (*eulabeia*); irrational Desire becomes rational **Will** (*boulesis*). Notice the missing fourth square: there is **no rational counterpart to Grief** (present evil), because to the sage, no external present event is an evil, and he never commits the only true evil (vice). **[INTERPRETATION: PRISCA THEOLOGIA / ESOTERIC]** In the esoteric physiology of the soul, Chrysippus's definitions—"irrational contraction (*systole*) of the mind" and "irrational elation/expansion (*eparsis*) of the mind"—are not figures of speech; they describe literal pneumatic physics. The soul-breath has a natural harmonic tension (*tonos*). When the mind ratifies a lie ("money is the supreme good" or "poverty is death"), that false axiom acts like a poison spell upon the *pneuma*, causing the subtle body to cramp, shrivel, or bloat out of its spherical equilibrium. Chronic false judgments calcify into "sicknesses of the soul" (*arrostemata*, like gout of the spirit). The transmutation of the four vulgar passions into the three *eupatheiai*—and the total erasure of the fourth quadrant, Grief—is the inner alchemy of the Porch: a soul that expands in Joy, steers by Caution, and reaches by Will, without ever contracting around present loss.
