[Why it matters]

Early Academic biographers claim Plato was conceived under Apollo's protection, born on Apollo's birthday, foretold to Socrates in a dream as a singing swan, and converted to philosophy by burning his tragic poems. [Note: Yonge prints "learnt" where the Greek *katephlexe* means "burnt," a known 1853 typesetting error for "burnt" confirmed by the invocation of Vulcan/fire.] Diogenes compiles a first-generation Academic hagiography—specifically citing Speusippus (Plato's nephew and successor) and Clearchus of Soli—designed to establish the founder's ontological status before a single doctrine is stated. In Greek antiquity, the swan is the sacred bird of Apollo, god of prophetic clarity, harmony, and purification; the seventh of Thargelion is the Delian festival of Apollo's birth. By linking Perictione's unviolated conception, the Delian birthday, and Socrates' dream of the fledgling swan taking flight from his lap, the early Academy constructs a genealogical warrant: Plato's philosophy is not mere human eristic, but an Apollonian transmission. The sacrifice of his tragic poems to Vulcan (Hephaestus) using a parodied Homeric hexameter marks the ritual death of mimetic, theatrical ambition so that the Socratic-Apollonian voice can emerge uncontaminated. [INTERPRETATION: PRISCA THEOLOGIA / ESOTERIC] In Renaissance Platonic reception, Marsilio Ficino and the Florentine Academy read Speusippus's nativity account not as partisan puffery, but as a literal record of solar-Apollonian influx. Within the *prisca theologia*, Apollo presides over the *mens* (the intuitive intellect) and the harmonic medicine of the soul—a link Diogenes himself cements in his epigram at III.30, pairing Asclepius (healer of bodies) with Plato (healer of immortal souls) as twin sons of Phoebus. The virgin-like restraint of Ariston indicates that a soul descending from the solar sphere requires an unpolluted, harmonically prepared vessel. Socrates' swan is the emblem of the initiate whose voice becomes sweetest at the threshold of physical death, signifying that Platonic dialectic is a pre-mortem separation of soul from body.
