LXVIII. A good state of affairs with reference to the laws, is divided under three heads. One when the laws are good, for that is a good state of affairs; so too is it, when the citizens abide by the existing laws; and the third case is, when although there are no positive laws, still men are good citizens in deference to custom and to established institutions; and this is also called a good state of affairs. So that of these three heads, one depends on the laws being good, another on obedience to existing laws, and the third on men yielding to good customs and institutions. So again, lawlessness is divided into three heads. One of which is, when the laws are bad, both as concerns strangers, and the citizens; another, when the citizens do not obey the laws that are established; and the third is when there is actually no law at all. So that one kind of lawlessness arises from bad laws, another from disobedience to existing laws, and the third from the absence of laws. — Diogenes Laertius, *Lives of Eminent Philosophers*, Book III, Section LXVIII, trans. Audit all three layers separately: (1) Are the formal rules themselves toxic? (2) Are good existing rules being ignored? (3) Has the unwritten cultural habitus collapsed so completely that no written rule can patch it?

[Use when]

Use when diagnosing why an organization, codebase, or civic institution is failing despite having a thick compliance manual—or succeeding despite having almost no formal documentation. ---

[Why it matters]

From the Aristotelian *Divisions* of Plato's oral teaching: civic order (*eunomia*) and civic breakdown (*anomia*) each occur in three distinct modes—through the quality of the code, the compliance of the people, or the presence/absence of unwritten custom. In the closing section of Book III (XLIV–LXXIV), Diogenes transcribes the *Diaireseis* (Divisions) attributed to Aristotle's notes on Plato's Academy. The method of *diairesis* cuts a generic concept into its exhaustive structural joints so a statesman or dialectician does not confuse distinct failure modes. Here, civic order (*eunomia*) and disorder (*anomia*) are mapped across three independent variables: (1) the formal statute's design, (2) behavioral compliance with existing statutes, and (3) the substratum of unwritten custom (*ethos* and *nomima*, cf. III.51). Crucially, a polis with zero written statutes can still enjoy *eunomia* if its habitual customs hold; conversely, a polis overflowing with written statutes is in a state of *anomia* (lawlessness) if those statutes are structurally predatory toward citizens and strangers. Bad law is classified not as harsh order, but as a species of lawlessness. [INTERPRETATION: PRISCA THEOLOGIA / ESOTERIC] Read through the lens of Renaissance political Platonism, this threefold division mirrors the descent of cosmic law (*lex divina*) into human polities. The highest, uncodified state—where citizens act rightly through internal custom without needing written statutes—corresponds to the Golden Age or Saturnian governance, where the law is written directly on the soul's habitus. Written statutes represent the Jovian or secondary remedial state, necessary when inward harmony dims. When written statutes themselves turn corrupt toward strangers and citizens, the city severs its link to cosmic justice (which III.44 defines as a "law of God") and becomes an inverted, tyrannical counterfeit—order in outward form, chaos in ontological substance.
