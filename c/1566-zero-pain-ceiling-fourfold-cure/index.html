[Why it matters]

Perfected beings neither feel anger nor grant favors; death is non-experience; maximum pleasure is simply the complete absence of pain; and bodily pain is either brief in its extremity or mild in its duration. These opening four maxims constitute what the Epicurean school termed the *tetrapharmakos* (the fourfold remedy), engineered to produce *ataraxia* (untroubledness of soul) and *aponia* (absence of bodily pain). Epicurus operates on strict Democritean atomism modified by sensory criteria: the soul is a corporeal aggregate of fine atoms that disperses at death, terminating all sensation (*aisthesis*). Because good and evil exist only in sensation, dissolution cancels the subject before death can be experienced. Equally radical is Maxim 3's structural definition of pleasure: against Cyrenaic hedonism, which treated pleasure as only kinetic motion, Epicurus establishes *katastematic* (static) pleasure as the absolute ceiling. Once want-induced pain is zeroed out, pleasure reaches its upper limit; further stimulation merely varies (*poikillei*) the state without increasing its magnitude. The gods, existing in blessed self-sufficiency between worlds, cannot act as cosmic magistrates because anger and favor require neediness and weakness. [INTERPRETATION: PRISCA THEOLOGIA / ESOTERIC] In the Renaissance reception of ancient philosophy, Marsilio Ficino (who burned his own juvenile Epicurean commentary on Lucretius before turning to Plato) treated Maxim 1's unmoved, passionless deity and Maxim 2's mortalism as the dark materialist foil against which the *prisca theologia* had to be defended. Yet within the Hermetic-Platonic chain, the first maxim's insistence that the divine is immune to wrath or transactional bribery was quietly absorbed by Renaissance syncretists (such as Giovanni Pico della Mirandola and later Giordano Bruno) to purge vulgar superstitious anthropomorphism from natural magic: the highest divine principle does not alter its emanations in response to emotional flattery, because cosmic sympathy operates by immutable structural law, not divine petulance. Likewise, the ceiling of katastematic rest was read by Christian-Stoic humanists like Pierre Gassendi as the shadow-image of contemplative *quies* — the soul's stillness once liberated from material agitation.
