The good to be most honoured and the greatest, he conceived it was not easy to discover, nor safe for those, who discovered it, to expose before all. To a very few then of his well-known friends, and those previously tried, did he give a share of his lectures on the good. If any one however takes up his writings carefully, (he will say that) he has laid down our good in the knowledge and contemplation of the primary good, which a person would call god and the primary mind. ... To all which he placed as an end, that was to follow, a similarity to god, as far as is possible. ... The end therefore would follow upon the beginning, or on the being assimilated to god; that god, to wit, who is in heaven, or, by Zeus, above heaven, and who does not possess virtue, but is better than it. From whence one would correctly say that misery is the evil-doing of a presiding genius, but happiness the good-doing... — Alcinous, *Handbook of Platonism*, Ch. 27, trans. George Burges (1854), pp. 293, 297–299. *Surface Gloss:* Plato guarded his teaching on the Supreme Good for tested initiates, locating it in contemplating the Primary Mind. Our ultimate goal is assimilation to God—specifically the God *in* heaven, not the God *above* heaven who transcends virtue altogether—making happiness the flourishing of our inner daimon. **Two-Tiered Assimilation (Don't Imitate the CEO's Desk, Imitate the Operating Officer):** When modeling yourself on a master or a supreme standard, distinguish between the principle *above* the system and the operator *inside* the system. A transcendent principle doesn't "practice discipline" because it has no friction to fight; if you try to imitate its effortless stillness before you've mastered your own friction, you'll just become lazy. Assimilate to the active operator in the field first.

[Why it matters]

Alcinous weaves together three monumental Platonic threads: the cautious reserve of *Timaeus* 28c / *Epistle* VII (along with the tradition of Plato's oral "Lecture on the Good"), the ethical *telos* of "assimilation to God so far as is possible" (*homoiosis theo kata to dynaton*, *Theaetetus* 176b), and the two-tiered theology established back in Chapter 10 of the *Handbook*. Notice the razor-sharp theological distinction at the climax: how can a human become "like God" through practicing virtue (prudence, justice, temperance), if the Supreme God—the First Mind (*protos nous*) *above* the heavens (*hyperouranios*)—has no passions to temper, no dangers to brave, and therefore "does not possess virtue, but is better than it" (*kreittonos hyparchontos tautes*, cf. Aristotle, *Nicomachean Ethics* VII.1)? Alcinous solves this by splitting the target of assimilation: through practical and political virtues, we assimilate ourselves to the **encosmic God** ("that god, to wit, who is in heaven," the Demiurge/World-Soul who actively governs the cosmos). Only by using the four mathematical sciences (*Music, Arithmetic, Astronomy, Geometry*) as "previous sacrifices and cleansings" (*proteleia kai prokatharsia*) do we awaken the internal daimon ("presiding genius," producing *eudaimonia*) to contemplate the hypercosmic First Mind. [INTERPRETATION: PRISCA THEOLOGIA / ESOTERIC] Burges (1854) remarks in Footnote 9 (p. 298) that the distinction between the god in heaven and the god above heaven who is superior to virtue "is not a Platonic, but rather a Neo-Platonic notion"—missing that Alcinous is here laying the exact 2nd-century bridge to Plotinus (*Enneads* I.2, "On Virtues"). Ficino's translation of this chapter (where he renders the mathematical sciences as necessary *si majoribus expiationibus opus fuerit*, "if there be need of greater expiations") cemented the Renaissance reading of Plato as an esoteric hierophant. Plato withheld the doctrine of the First Good from the public because uninitiated minds confuse relative goods (health, wealth, power) with the Absolute. Separated from virtue, worldly blessings are mere "matter" (*hyle*) that becomes actively toxic in the hands of a fool. True initiation proceeds in two degrees: Lesser Mysteries (civic virtue, aligning the soul with the cosmic Demiurge) and Greater Mysteries (mathematical-noetic catharsis, uniting the "eye of the soul" with the hypercosmic Father who sits above all moral duality).
