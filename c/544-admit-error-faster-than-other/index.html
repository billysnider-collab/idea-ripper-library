"When we are wrong, we may admit it to ourselves; and if we are handled gently and tactfully, we may admit it to others and even take pride in our frankness and broadmindedness; but not if someone else is trying to ram the unpalatable fact down our throats." The move is preemptive: "If you are wrong, admit it quickly and emphatically." Carnegie's field case is literal — accused by a policeman of walking his dog off-leash, he condemned himself before the officer could: "You're absolutely right. It's inexcusable" — and the officer, with nothing left to prosecute, dropped it. The mechanism: "By fighting, you never get enough, but by yielding, you get more than you expected."

— In our words · Source: How to Win Friends and Influence People (Dale Carnegie)

[Use when]

You're caught and the other side is loading up — beat them to the sentence with a faster, more emphatic admission.

[Why it matters]

Self-accusation disarms accusation: you steal the other person's ammunition by firing it yourself. Pride is protected — yours is displayed as frankness, theirs as magnanimity — and the conflict evaporates for lack of an opponent.
