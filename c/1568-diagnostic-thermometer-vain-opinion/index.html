“30. “Of the desires, some are natural and necessary, some natural, but not necessary, and some are neither natural nor necessary, but owe their existence to vain opinions.” (Epicurus thinks that those are natural and necessary which put an end to pains, as drink when one is thirsty; and that those are natural but not necessary which only diversify pleasure, but do not remove pain, such as expensive food; and that these are neither natural nor necessary, which are such as crowns, or the erection of statues.) 31. “Those desires which do not lead to pain, if they are not satisfied, are not necessary. It is easy to impose silence on them when they appear difficult to gratify, or likely to produce injury. 32. “When the natural desires, the failing to satisfy which is, nevertheless, not painful, are violent and obstinate, it is a proof that there is an admixture of vain opinion in them; for then energy does not arise from their own nature, but from the vain opinions of men.” — Diogenes Laertius, *Lives of Eminent Philosophers*, Book X, Sec. The **Obsession Diagnostic**: how do you tell whether an intense craving (for a specific hire, a luxury acquisition, a romantic target, or a market win) is a real structural need or an ego-hallucination? Run the two-step Epicurean test: First, if I walk away right now, does it cause actual structural damage ("pain"), or just a missed variation? [Why it matters]

Desires split into three classes: natural-necessary (ending pain), natural-unnecessary (merely varying pleasure), and unnatural-unnecessary (status symbols). When a non-painful natural desire feels violent and obsessive, its heat comes from corrupted social opinion, not nature. Epicurus constructs a three-tier sorting sieve for human motivation, anchored by a somatic test: *does non-fulfillment cause physical pain?* Class 1 (natural and necessary, like basic water and bread) removes bodily pain (*aponia*) and is finite and cheap. Class 3 (neither natural nor necessary, like civic crowns and honorific statues) is pure *kenodoxia* ("vain opinion") — boundless, socially fabricated, and inherently destabilizing. The diagnostic brilliance sits in Maxim 32's analysis of Class 2 (natural but unnecessary desires, such as luxurious food or sexual attraction). By nature, Class 2 desires merely diversify pleasure without removing pain; therefore, left to themselves, they are mild and easily dismissed when costly (Maxim 31). Whenever a person experiences a Class 2 desire as "violent and obstinate" (*suntonoi*), Epicurus diagnoses an epistemic contamination: the intensity is not biological drive, but *kenodoxia* (mental fiction and status-delusion) parasitical upon a natural appetite. [INTERPRETATION: PRISCA THEOLOGIA / ESOTERIC] Note first that the parenthetical gloss attached to Maxim 30 in Yonge's text is an ancient scholiast's note incorporated into the manuscript tradition and translated by Yonge (1853). Within the Renaissance esoteric tradition, this Epicurean diagnostic of *kenodoxia* (empty phantasy) was mapped onto the Platonic-Hermetic doctrine of the *imagination* (*phantasia*) as the seat of demonic and astral glamour. For Ficino and Agrippa, the lower soul becomes enslaved to material fetters not through the body's simple vegetative needs, which are quiet and self-limiting, but when the unruly *phantasia* projects infinite, illusory grandeur onto finite sensible objects. What Epicurus calls the "admixture of vain opinion" inflating a mild natural appetite into violent obsession was read by Renaissance moralists (including Montaigne and Erasmus) as the exact mechanism by which collective social delusion magnetizes a harmless material object into an idol.
