Rules for Radicals

Saul D. Alinsky · Nonfiction · 17 rips

Power tactics — Alinsky's 1971 organizer's manual: the thirteen rules of power tactics, the ethics of means and ends, the haves and have-nots, and the organizer who builds power for others to use.

Always remember the first rule of power tactics: Power is not only what you have but what the enemy thinks you have.

Why it matters: Power is perception: the size of your force is measured in the enemy's head, not in your headcount.

When you're outnumbered and need to look bigger than you are.

The second rule is: Never go outside the experience of your people. When an action or tactic is outside the experience of the people, the result is confusion, fear, and retreat. It also means a collapse of communication, as we have noted.

Why it matters: Novelty in tactics backfires on your own side first — your people retreat before the enemy even reacts.

Before trying a tactic your people have never seen before.

The third rule is: Wherever possible go outside of the experience of the enemy. Here you want to cause confusion, fear, and retreat.

Why it matters: The mirror of rule two: unfamiliar tactics are a weapon only when pointed at the other side.

When you're planning how the enemy will react to something new.

The fourth rule is: Make the enemy live up to their own book of rules. You can kill them with this, for they can no more obey their own rules than the Christian church can live up to Christianity.

Why it matters: No institution can survive full compliance with its own stated standards — so demand exactly that.

When an institution preaches one thing and practices another.

The fourth rule carries within it the fifth rule: Ridicule is man's most potent weapon. It is almost impossible to counterattack ridicule. Also it infuriates the opposition, who then react to your advantage.

Why it matters: Laughter can't be answered with a press release — and an angry opponent makes your mistakes for you.

When the other side's dignity is its armor.

The sixth rule is: A good tactic is one that your people enjoy. If your people are not having a ball doing it, there is something very wrong with the tactic.

Why it matters: Fun is a tactical diagnostic — enjoyment sustains action longer than duty does.

When participation is flagging and you can't figure out why.

The seventh rule: A tactic that drags on too long becomes a drag. Man can sustain militant interest in any issue for only a limited time, after which it becomes a ritualistic commitment, like going to church on Sunday mornings.

Why it matters: Energy has a shelf life — a stale tactic keeps the form of action while the life drains out of it.

When a campaign is running on habit instead of heat.

The eighth rule: Keep the pressure on, with different tactics and actions, and utilize all events of the period for your purpose.

Why it matters: Relief is the enemy's recovery time — variety of attack keeps the opposition from ever resetting.

When the other side is hoping you'll get tired.

The ninth rule: The threat is usually more terrifying than the thing itself.

Why it matters: Imagination does the work of force — the enemy's fear of what you might do outruns anything you could actually do.

When you have leverage but not the strength to spend it.

The tenth rule: The major premise for tactics is the development of operations that will maintain a constant pressure upon the opposition. It is this unceasing pressure that results in the reactions from the opposition that are essential for the success of the campaign.

Why it matters: The action is in the reaction — you don't win exchanges, you keep the enemy reacting, because reactions are what you steer.

When you're designing a campaign as a sequence, not a single event.

The eleventh rule is: If you push a negative hard and deep enough it will break through into its counterside; this is based on the principle that every positive has its negative.

Why it matters: Overextension flips: push the enemy's attack far enough and it becomes your asset — panic makes them burglarize your office and leave their fingerprints everywhere.

When the opposition is overreaching and about to discredit itself.

The twelfth rule: The price of a successful attack is a constructive alternative. You cannot risk being trapped by the enemy in his sudden agreement with your demand and saying “You’re right—we don’t know what to do about this issue. Now you tell us.”

Why it matters: Winning the argument without an answer hands you the problem — surrender can be the enemy's best counterattack.

Before you demand something, have the replacement ready.

The thirteenth rule: Pick the target, freeze it, personalize it, and polarize it.

Why it matters: Institutions survive by diffusing responsibility until attack is impossible — so you pin one name to the evil and never let it squirm free.

When everyone's responsible and therefore no one is.

The man of action views the issue of means and ends in pragmatic and strategic terms. He has no other problem; he thinks only of his actual resources and the possibilities of various choices of action. He asks of ends only whether they are achievable and worth the cost; of means, only whether they will work. To say that corrupt means corrupt the ends is to believe in the immaculate conception of ends and principles. The real arena is corrupt and bloody.

Why it matters: Alinsky's ethics: judge tactics by whether they work and whether the end is worth it — purity of means is a luxury of observers, not actors.

When someone demands clean hands in a dirty fight.

Mankind has been and is divided into three parts: the Haves, the Have-Nots, and the Have-a-Little, Want Mores.

Why it matters: Every power fight runs on this trinity — the comfortable freeze the status quo, the desperate want to take, and the middle class wants change without risk.

When you're mapping who actually has a stake in changing things.

This is the basic difference between the leader and the organizer. The leader goes on to build power to fulfill his desires, to hold and wield the power for purposes both social and personal. He wants power himself. The organizer finds his goal in creation of power for others to use.

Why it matters: Leadership accumulates power; organizing distributes it — the organizer's product is other people's power, not his own.

When you're tempted to become the leader instead of the builder.

They were not being attacked or insulted—they were being laughed at, and this was insufferable.
