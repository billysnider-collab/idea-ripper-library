The Art of Public Speaking

by J. Berg Esenwein & Dale Carnegie

25 rips · Persuading · Negotiating

Public speaking — the 1915 textbook Carnegie co-wrote before How to Win Friends: stage fright, emphasis, pauses, notes, suggestion and moving a crowd.

Mechanisms (24)

mechanism #1088

Pasture the horse beside the tracks

The opening chapter's cure for stage fright is repeated exposure.

"How would you cure a horse that is afraid of cars—graze him in a back-woods lot where he would never see steam-engines or automobiles, or drive or pasture him where he would frequently see the machines?" "You must learn to speak by speaking."

Why it matters

The opening chapter's cure for stage fright is repeated exposure. Books can coach you, but in their words "no one ever learns to swim" in a wetless bathing suit.

Use when: You've been reading about public speaking instead of booking a talk.

mechanism #1091

Whisper the word that matters

Emphasis comes from contrast.

"To make a word emphatic, deliver it differently from the manner in which the words surrounding it are delivered. If you have been talking loudly, utter the emphatic word in a concentrated whisper—and you have intense emphasis."

Why it matters

Emphasis comes from contrast. A Fifth Avenue bus turns heads in a farm town and an ox team turns heads on Fifth Avenue, so whatever you've been doing, change it on the key word.

Use when: You've been raising your voice to stress points and the room has tuned out.

mechanism #1092

Stress the man who bit the dog

Words the audience already expects don't need stress.

"'But,' said Mr. Dana, 'if you see a man bite a dog, hurry back to the office and write the story.'" "'New stars and suns' are hardly as emphatic as the word 'larger.' Why? Because we expect an astronomer to discover heavenly bodies rather than cooking recipes."

Why it matters

Words the audience already expects don't need stress. Save emphasis for the new or surprising word, the part that is news. The authors treat the average sentence as "just one big word, with the important word as the accented syllable," the way nobody stresses every syllable of "Massachusetts."

Use when: You're marking up a script and stressing the obvious nouns.

mechanism #1093

Stress one word and they hear its opposite

Heavy stress implies a contrast.

"If you say, 'My horse is not black,' what color immediately comes into mind? White, naturally, for that is the opposite of black." "Strongly emphasizing a single word has a tendency to suggest its antithesis."

Why it matters

Heavy stress implies a contrast. Leaning on "chance" in "Destiny is not a matter of chance" sets up "choice" before you say it.

Use when: You want the audience to arrive at your point a beat before you state it.

mechanism #1094

Pause on both sides of the big phrase

The authors separate this from stumbling with "uh" and "er." A deliberate pause makes the audience wait and makes the phrase land.

"one of the most important means of developing power in public speaking is to pause either before or after, or both before and after, an important word or phrase."

Why it matters

The authors separate this from stumbling with "uh" and "er." A deliberate pause makes the audience wait and makes the phrase land. They say it is hard to grasp until you notice good speakers doing it.

Use when: Your key line goes by before anyone registers it.

Why it matters

Uniform text is hard to find with a half-second glance. Visual marks act as handles, and the authors say even an accidental blot can help you recall a point.

mechanism #1096

Memorize the joints, improvise the rest

Write and memorize the introduction, the conclusion and the key argument, and speak the rest.

"Others again write and commit a few important parts of the address—the introduction, the conclusion, some vital argument, some pat illustration—and depend on the hour for the language of the rest."

Why it matters

Write and memorize the introduction, the conclusion and the key argument, and speak the rest. Fully memorized lines, the authors warn, usually "sound like memorized words, and repel."

Use when: You're choosing between reading a script and winging it.

Running over takes time from the next speaker and the audience.

"Let ten minutes mean neither nine nor eleven—though better nine than eleven, at all events." "Blessed is the man that maketh short speeches, for he shall be invited to speak again."

Why it matters

Running over takes time from the next speaker and the audience. The fix is planning: give each point its share of time and decide in advance where the main section sits.

mechanism #1103

Suggest their minds are already changing

The authors say feelings, thoughts and wills "tend to follow the line of least resistance." Telling people they are dug in digs them in; suggesting that opinion is already shifting makes holding out the effort.

"Suggest to a man that it is impossible to change his mind and in most cases it becomes more difficult to do so" "Suggest subtly and persistently that the opinions of those in the audience who are opposed to your views are changing"

Why it matters

The authors say feelings, thoughts and wills "tend to follow the line of least resistance." Telling people they are dug in digs them in; suggesting that opinion is already shifting makes holding out the effort. They note the born contrarian needs the reverse.

Use when: You're addressing people who are against you and about to call them stubborn.

mechanism #1107

Start with their pains

Advertising that opened with the firm's size or the remedy's strength went unread.

"Patent medicine advertisement usually begins by talking about your pains—they begin on your interests." "Recently a book-salesman entered an attorney's office in New York and inquired: 'Do you want to buy a book?' Had the lawyer wanted a book he would probably have bought one without waiting for a book-salesman to call."

Why it matters

Advertising that opened with the firm's size or the remedy's strength went unread. Opening with the reader's problem made them ask for the solution. The authors set it against a book salesman whose opening, "Do you want to buy a book?", talked only in terms of his own interest.

Use when: Your pitch opens with your company's history.

mechanism #1109

Repeat their claim while your facts take it apart

The authors print Antony's oration as their model of turning listeners into a crowd.

"For Brutus is an honorable man, So are they all, all honorable men" "He hath brought many captives home to Rome, Whose ransoms did the general coffers fill: Did this in Caesar seem ambitious?"

Why it matters

The authors print Antony's oration as their model of turning listeners into a crowd. Antony never attacks Brutus's claim head-on. He repeats it after each fact (the ransoms, the refused crown, the tears for the poor) until the refrain itself sounds like an accusation.

Use when: You have to answer a respected opponent without attacking them by name.

mechanism #1111

Start on the motive they have, end on the highest

The authors use a Kipling war speech that opens on self-interest and ends on patriotism.

"Notice how at the last the pleader sweeps on to the highest ground he can take." "This is a notable example of progressive appeal, beginning with a low motive and ending with a high one in such a way as to carry all the force of prejudice yet gain all the value of patriotic fervor."

Why it matters

The authors use a Kipling war speech that opens on self-interest and ends on patriotism. They call the appeal to prejudice rarely justifiable and still show the sequence: meet people at the motive they already feel, then climb.

Use when: Your audience cares about their own costs and you need them to care about the larger cause.

argument #1102

Don't tell them not to put beans up their noses

Naming an action plants it, even inside a prohibition.

"To warn children that they should not put beans up their noses is the surest method of getting them to do it." "Every thought called up in the mind of your audience will work either for or against you."

Why it matters

Naming an action plants it, even inside a prohibition. The authors say a speaker should avoid calling up the very thoughts that work against the goal.

Use when: Your warning slide lists the exact mistakes you don't want people to make.
