## Steal

All warfare is based on deception. Hence, when able to attack, we must seem unable; when using our forces, we must seem inactive; when we are near, we must make the enemy believe we are far away; when far away, we must make him believe we are near.

## Why it matters

This is the book's master key, stated in chapter one before a single tactic. Everything in the thirteen chapters is downstream of it: the feigned disorder, the baits, the secrecy, the spies. The point is not that lying is clever; it is that the enemy's decisions are made from his picture of you, so the picture is the battlefield. Control what he believes and you control what he does, before a blow is landed. Hold out baits to entice him, feign disorder and crush him, pretend to be weak that he may grow arrogant. The fight starts in his head.

## Use when

Someone is optimizing their own strength and ignoring what the other side believes about them.

## Steal

Hence to fight and conquer in all your battles is not supreme excellence; supreme excellence consists in breaking the enemy's resistance without fighting. The best thing of all is to take the enemy's country whole and intact; to shatter and destroy it is not so good.

## Why it matters

The book's verdict on itself, and it inverts the hero story. The celebrated general who wins every battle is second-rate; the supreme artist takes the country intact, the army uncaptured, the cities unsieged. Destruction is a failure of imagination: every battle actually fought is a plan that didn't work earlier, when it was cheaper. The ranking is explicit. The highest form of generalship is to balk the enemy's plans; next, to prevent the junction of his forces; next, to attack his army in the field; and the worst policy of all is to besiege walled cities, where a third of your men can die and the town still stands.

## Use when

Victory is being measured in battles won instead of costs avoided.

## Steal

If you know the enemy and know yourself, you need not fear the result of a hundred battles. If you know yourself but not the enemy, for every victory gained you will also suffer a defeat. If you know neither the enemy nor yourself, you will succumb in every battle.

## Why it matters

The famous formula is only the headline; the book gives you the machinery twice. First the audit: five constant factors, seven head-to-head comparisons of the two sides, and the startling claim, "By means of these seven considerations I can forecast victory or defeat." Victory is not luck or genius; it is a calculation made "in the temple ere the battle is fought." Then, in the closing chapter, the source of the decisive input: "what enables the wise sovereign and the good general to strike and conquer, and achieve things beyond the reach of ordinary men, is foreknowledge," and "Knowledge of the enemy's dispositions can only be obtained from other men," not from spirits, not from experience, not from calculation. And the sting: "to remain in ignorance of the enemy's condition simply because one grudges the outlay of a hundred ounces of silver in honors and emoluments, is the height of inhumanity." Intelligence is the cheapest victory in the book.

## Use when

A decision is being made on assumptions nobody paid to check.

## Steal

Thus it is that in war the victorious strategist only seeks battle after the victory has been won, whereas he who is destined to defeat first fights and afterwards looks for victory. He wins his battles by making no mistakes. Making no mistakes is what establishes the certainty of victory, for it means conquering an enemy that is already defeated.

## Why it matters

The coldest sentence in the book. The battle is not where victory is created; it is where a victory arranged beforehand is collected. The good fighter "first put themselves beyond the possibility of defeat, and then waited for an opportunity of defeating the enemy," and the opportunity is provided by the enemy himself. Your half of the work is entirely negative: become undefeatable. The positive half, the enemy's mistake, cannot be manufactured, only awaited. That is why the book despises the spectacular win: "To see victory only when it is within the ken of the common herd is not the acme of excellence." If everyone can see why you won, you won late.

## Use when

Someone wants the decisive confrontation now, before the groundwork exists.

## Steal

There is no instance of a country having benefited from prolonged warfare. Though we have heard of stupid haste in war, cleverness has never been seen associated with long delays.

## Why it matters

Chapter two is an economics lecture disguised as strategy. A hundred thousand men cost a thousand ounces of silver a day; prices rise near the army and drain the people; the peasantry is stripped by exactions; the state's revenue bleeds into broken chariots and worn-out horses. Then the strategic consequence: "when your weapons are dulled, your ardor damped, your strength exhausted and your treasure spent, other chieftains will spring up to take advantage of your extremity." Time is not neutral; it taxes the attacker and invites third parties. Hence "let your great object be victory, not lengthy campaigns." Speed is not impatience. It is cost control, and the leader of armies is "the arbiter of the people's fate."

## Use when

A campaign, project, or conflict is drifting past its planned end and everyone is calling it patience.

## Steal

Military tactics are like unto water; for water in its natural course runs away from high places and hastens downwards. Therefore, just as water retains no constant shape, so in warfare there are no constant conditions.

## Why it matters

The book's anti-doctrine doctrine, and its answer to everyone who wants Sun Tzu as a checklist. "Do not repeat the tactics which have gained you one victory, but let your methods be regulated by the infinite variety of circumstances." Water avoids the high ground and strikes the low; the soldier avoids the strong and strikes the weak, shaping the victory to the foe in front of him. The highest praise in the book goes to "a heaven-born captain": one who can modify his tactics in relation to his opponent and thereby win. The method is to have no fixed method. A playbook is a record of what worked against someone else.

## Use when

A winning playbook is being rerun against a different opponent.

## Steal

Therefore the clever combatant imposes his will on the enemy, but does not allow the enemy's will to be imposed on him. We can form a single united body, while the enemy must split up into fractions. If he sends reinforcements everywhere, he will everywhere be weak.

## Why it matters

The leverage chapter. You do not need to be stronger overall; you need to be stronger at the single point of contact, and you get that by making the enemy defend everywhere. Conceal where you will strike, "for then the enemy will have to prepare against a possible attack at several different points," and his strength spreads thin against possibilities while yours concentrates against a certainty. "Numerical weakness comes from having to prepare against possible attacks; numerical strength, from compelling our adversary to make these preparations against us." Uncertainty is a weapon, and you aim it at the enemy's deployments: threaten what he holds dear and he will come to you, on your terms, divided.

## Use when

Facing a stronger opponent: don't match him everywhere, make him guard everywhere.

## Steal

There are three ways in which a ruler can bring misfortune upon his army: by commanding the army to advance or to retreat, being ignorant of the fact that it cannot obey. This is called hobbling the army. If fighting is sure to result in victory, then you must fight, even though the ruler forbid it; if fighting will not result in victory, then you must not fight even at the ruler's bidding.

## Why it matters

The book's political theory, and its most subversive claim: the sovereign's interference is a defeat mechanism, cataloged like terrain. Ordering an advance the ground cannot support, governing an army like a civilian bureaucracy, promoting officers without regard for circumstance: each "shakes the confidence of the soldiers," and together they are "simply bringing anarchy into the army, and flinging victory away." The remedy is stated without flinching. The general who knows the ground must disobey. And the portrait of the one worth trusting: "The general who advances without coveting fame and retreats without fearing disgrace, whose only thought is to protect his country and do good service for his sovereign, is the jewel of the kingdom." Loyalty to the mission outranks obedience to the order.

## Use when

Distant authority is issuing confident orders about ground it has never seen.

## Steal

Throw your soldiers into positions whence there is no escape, and they will prefer death to flight. Place your army in deadly peril, and it will survive; plunge it into desperate straits, and it will come off in safety. He burns his boats and breaks his cooking-pots.

## Why it matters

The psychology chapter, and the book is clinical about it. Soldiers with an exit use it; soldiers with no exit become, in the book's cold phrase, capable of "striking a blow for victory" precisely because they have fallen into harm's way. On desperate ground the troops "lose the sense of fear," stand firm without orders, and fight hard because there is no help for it. The general is even told to keep his own men ignorant of the design: "Confront your soldiers with the deed itself; never let them know your design," and to "mystify his officers and men by false reports and appearances, and thus keep them in total ignorance." It is manipulation as a technology of courage. Disturbing, deliberate, and presented as the difference between an army that dissolves and one that holds.

## Use when

A team keeps one foot out the door and wonders why it can't commit.

## Steal

No ruler should put troops into the field merely to gratify his own spleen; no general should fight a battle simply out of pique. Anger may in time change to gladness; vexation may be succeeded by content. But a kingdom that has once been destroyed can never come again into being; nor can the dead ever be brought back to life.

## Why it matters

The brake at the end of a book about winning. After thirteen chapters of deception, manipulation, and fire, the closing moral is restraint: "Move not unless you see an advantage; use not your troops unless there is something to be gained; fight not unless the position is critical." Emotions are reversible; consequences are not. That asymmetry is the whole argument: anger passes, kingdoms don't return. So "the enlightened ruler is heedful and the good general full of caution," and "this is the way to keep a country at peace and an army intact." The treatise bends back to its first sentence, that war is "a matter of life and death, a road either to safety or to ruin," and therefore never an instrument of mood.

## Use when

Anger or pride is drafting the battle plan.
