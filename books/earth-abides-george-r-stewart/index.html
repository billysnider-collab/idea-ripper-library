Card #c1418Synthesis

Coins into arrowheads: value is what the next generation can use

Hand down what your successors can use, in the form they will use it, not the artifact you revere.

— In our words · Source: Earth Abides (George R. Stewart)

[Use when]

Handing a codebase to a new team: give them the runbook and working scripts they'll touch daily, not the archive of design history.

[Why it matters]

The tribe's young hunter pounds old coins into arrowheads, so the old world's counted money survives only as raw material. Ish preserved knowledge in its old forms; what lasted was what fit the next generation's hands.

Card #c1419Synthesis

The Year of the Schoolteaching: teach what the environment rewards

Teach what the learner's environment will actually test, or expect the lesson to lapse.

— In our words · Source: Earth Abides (George R. Stewart)

[Use when]

Designing onboarding: tie every module to a task the new hire will be judged on in their first month.

[Why it matters]

Ish's school (reading, writing, arithmetic, geography) fades while hunting skills thrive, because the new world rewards only what keeps people fed. Learner incentives outrank teacher effort.

Card #c1420Synthesis

The hammer and the bow: the founder's tools become relics

Separate your mission from your favorite tool, and let your successors swap the tool.

— In our words · Source: Earth Abides (George R. Stewart)

[Use when]

You're the founder defending a tool or process the team has quietly stopped using: ask whether it serves the mission or only your identity.

[Why it matters]

Ish's four-pound single-jack hammer ties him to the old world, while the young men prefer bows to rifles that sometimes just go "click." The mission (survival, continuity) passes down; the founder's instrument survives only as a relic.

Card #c1421Synthesis

Men go and come, but earth abides: decide on the long timescale

Judge a heavy decision on the longest relevant timescale, not your ego's.

— In our words · Source: Earth Abides (George R. Stewart)

[Use when]

You're agonizing over whether a project will be remembered: ask instead whether it keeps the people who depend on it fed and working next year.

[Why it matters]

Dying, Ish looks at the unchanged hills and stops grading his life by what he built: "Men go and come, but earth abides." Most strategic anguish comes from scoring yourself on a clock the world doesn't use.
