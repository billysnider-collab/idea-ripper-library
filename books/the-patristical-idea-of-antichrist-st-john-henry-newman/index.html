The Patristical Idea of Antichrist

St. John Henry Newman · Hermetica · 10 rips

Four lectures, 1838 — Newman's patristic survey of Antichrist from the Tracts for the Times: his times, his religion, his city, his persecution.

Do you think he is so unskilful in his craft, as to ask you openly and plainly to join him in his warfare against the Truth? No; he offers you baits to tempt you. He promises you civil liberty; he promises you equality; he promises you trade and wealth; he promises you a remission of taxes; he promises you reform. He offers you knowledge, science, philosophy, enlargement of mind. He scoffs at times gone by; he scoffs at every institution which reveres them. He bids you mount aloft. He shows you how to become as gods. Then he laughs and jokes with you, and gets intimate with you; he takes your hand, and gets his fingers between yours, and grasps them, and then you are his.

Why it matters: Newman refuses the cartoon of evil as frontal assault. The danger is not a demand to renounce the truth — it is the flattering offer that costs nothing today: liberty, wealth, knowledge, reform. Seduction works because each bait is a genuine good detached from its end; the hand closes finger by finger, and by the time you notice the grip, the consent is already given.

Use when a compromise arrives dressed as progress. Use when weighing whether a harmless-looking concession is the first finger between yours. Use when the threat does not look like a threat.

The Man of Sin is born of an Apostasy, or at least comes into power through an apostasy, or is preceded by an apostasy, or would not be except for an apostasy. The Israelites, or at least great numbers of them, put off their own sacred religion, and then the enemy was allowed to come in.

Why it matters: Newman's reading of St. Paul reverses the usual fear: the Antichrist is not an outside invader overpowering a faithful people — he is the product of an inside hollowing. The falling away comes first; the tyrant only harvests what the apostasy planted. Evil's power is downstream of the faithful's surrender.

Use when diagnosing how a bad regime or movement actually rose — look for the prior internal collapse, not the external force. Use when tempted to blame the invader instead of the open gate.

Every event of this world is a type of those that follow, history proceeding forward as a circle ever enlarging. The days of the Apostles typified the last days: there were false Christs, and risings, and troubles, and persecutions. In like manner, every age presents its own picture of those still future events.

Why it matters: Newman gives a way to read the news without hysteria or complacency: history rhymes because it spirals — each age stages a small, legible version of the final things. Antiochus, Julian, and Mahomet are not the Antichrist, but they are his rehearsals. The pattern is real; the scale is what grows.

Use when a present crisis feels unprecedented — look for the smaller type in the past that already played this scene. Use when someone cries that this is the end, or that this has never happened before.

Little children, it is the last time; and as ye have heard that the Antichrist shall come, even now are there many Antichrists; whereby we know that it is the last time. Antichrist was come, and was not come; it was, and it was not the last time.

Why it matters: Newman holds the paradox St. John forces on every generation: the spirit is already at work — the mystery of iniquity doth already work — and yet the final form is still future. This kills both errors at once: the complacency that says nothing is happening, and the date-setting that says it is happening now. Every age lives in the overlap.

Use when the signs are real but the conclusion is premature. Use when someone is either asleep or panicked — the text holds both truths at once.

It was the judicial punishment of the Jews, as of all unbelievers in one way or another, that, having rejected the true Christ, they should take up with a false one.

Why it matters: Newman's hardest doctrine in the lectures: unbelief does not leave you empty — it leaves you occupied. Rejection of the true is not a neutral vacancy; it is a judgment that takes the form of being handed over to the counterfeit. The mind that refuses the real Christ does not stay free; it becomes available to whoever comes in his own name.

Use when watching people who left a faith or a cause and then attached themselves to something worse. Use when the emptiness left by rejection keeps getting filled by impostors.

After having broken away from all restraint as regards God and man, they gave a name to that reprobate state itself into which they had thrown themselves, and exalted it, that very negation of religion, or rather that real and living blasphemy, into a kind of god. They called it Liberty, and they literally worshipped it as a divinity. They appointed festivals besides in honour of Reason, the Country, the Constitution, and the Virtues.

Why it matters: Newman's exhibit from the French Revolution: men who abjured every worship could not abide the vacuum — they canonized their own negation. The pattern is permanent: whatever dethrones God gets enthroned in His place, even if it has to be an abstraction. Iconoclasm always ends in a new altar.

Use when a movement claims to have abolished worship and then starts holding festivals. Use when the rational replacement for religion starts demanding the devotion religion used to get.

Infidelity leads to superstition, and the men most reckless in their blasphemy are cowards also as regards the invisible world.

Why it matters: Newman resolves Daniel's apparent contradiction — Antichrist both opposes all worship and institutes a strange god — with a law of human nature: the fiercer the unbelief, the more abject the superstition it breeds. You cannot kill the religious instinct; you can only redirect it, and the redirected version is cruder than what it replaced.

Use when the loudest skeptic in the room turns out to be the most superstitious. Use when a disenchanted age starts consulting horoscopes, algorithms, and gurus.

The Church sanctifies, yet suffers with, the world, sharing its sufferings, yet lightening them. Babylon was a mere prison of the Church; Rome had received her as a guest. For the sake of the Christians in it, Rome was chastised, not overthrown utterly.

Why it matters: Newman's answer to why Rome was not annihilated like Babylon: a city that hosts the Church gets Abraham's bargain — the righteous in it stay the judgment. The Church does not escape the world's plagues; she absorbs them and dilutes them, salt in the wound of history. Presence, not distance, is what restrains the wrath.

Use when wondering whether to withdraw from a corrupt place or stay in it. Use when the faithful are suffering alongside the guilty and need to know the suffering is doing something.

In persecution the Church begins and in persecution she ends. He left her in persecution, and He will find her in persecution. You might set down persecution as one of the peculiarities by which you recognize her. Blessed are they who are persecuted for righteousness' sake — a sort of sign and token of His followers.

Why it matters: Newman makes persecution a mark of identity, not an accident of history: the Church that is never opposed has likely ceased to be recognizable. Comfort is the anomaly; opposition is the signature. This inverts the instinct to measure the Church's health by her acceptance.

Use when a community's respectability starts looking like its goal. Use when suffering for the faith feels like failure — Newman says it is the badge.

After all perhaps it may not be a persecution of blood and death, but of craft and subtlety only. Satan may adopt the more alarming weapons of deceit — he may hide himself — he may attempt to seduce us in little things, and so to move Christians, not all at once, but by little and little from their true position. It is his policy to split us up and divide us, to dislodge us gradually from off our rock of strength.

Why it matters: Newman's most modern note: the last danger may not look like scaffolds but like slow erosion — division, dependence on the world for protection, surrender of independence by little and little. A persecution you can photograph is easier to resist than one that arrives as a series of reasonable accommodations.

Use when the threat is not violence but dilution. Use when each individual compromise looks defensible and only the direction is damning.
