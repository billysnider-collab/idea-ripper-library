Manufacturing Consent

Noam Chomsky & Edward S. Herman · Nonfiction · 15 rips

Propaganda model — Chomsky and Herman's 1988 classic on the five news filters, worthy and unworthy victims, and how consent is manufactured without censorship.

The essential ingredients of our propaganda model, or set of news “filters,” fall under the following headings: (1) the size, concentrated ownership, owner wealth, and profit orientation of the dominant mass-media firms; (2) advertising as the primary income source of the mass media; (3) the reliance of the media on information provided by government, business, and “experts” funded and approved by these primary sources and agents of power; (4) “flak” as a means of disciplining the media; and (5) “anticommunism” as a national religion and control mechanism. The raw material of news must pass through successive filters, leaving only the cleansed residue fit to print.

Why it matters: The entire model in one paragraph: five structural filters decide what counts as news before any reporter writes a word.

When someone asks how propaganda works in a country with no censorship.

The elite domination of the media and marginalization of dissidents that results from the operation of these filters occurs so naturally that media news people, frequently operating with complete integrity and goodwill, are able to convince themselves that they choose and interpret the news “objectively” and on the basis of professional news values.

Why it matters: No conspiracy is required: sincere journalists can report objectively within limits they never chose.

When “but reporters mean well” is used to dismiss media critique.

Ben Bagdikian stresses the fact that despite the large media numbers, the twenty-nine largest media systems account for over half of the output of newspapers, and most of the sales and audiences in magazines, broadcasting, books, and movies. He contends that these “constitute a new Private Ministry of Information and Culture” that can set the national agenda.

Why it matters: Twenty-five thousand media entities, twenty-nine setting the agenda — ownership concentration as the first filter, with 1986 numbers.

When the sheer number of outlets is offered as proof of press freedom.

In short, the mass media are interested in attracting audiences with buying power, not audiences per se; it is affluent audiences that spark advertiser interest today, as in the nineteenth century. The idea that the drive for large audiences makes the mass media “democratic” thus suffers from the initial weakness that its political analogue is a voting system weighted by income!

Why it matters: The advertising filter's core logic: the audience is the product, and only affluent buyers count.

When reach and ratings get mistaken for democratic representation.

Barnouw notes that although at that time a great many large companies were spending money on commercials and other publicity regarding environmental problems, the documentary series failed for want of sponsors. The problem was one of excessive objectivity in the series, which included suggestions of corporate or systemic failure, whereas the corporate message “was one of reassurance.”

Why it matters: An NBC environmental series died not for bias but for objectivity — sponsors wanted reassurance, not systemic failure.

When someone asks how advertisers shape content without issuing orders.

The mass media are drawn into a symbiotic relationship with powerful sources of information by economic necessity and reciprocity of interest. The media need a steady, reliable flow of the raw material of news. They have daily news demands and imperative news schedules that they must meet.

Why it matters: The sourcing filter: beats, press conferences, and official leaks are cheaper than reporting — so power becomes the default source.

When official sources dominate a story and nobody asks why.

Mark Fishman calls this “the principle of bureaucratic affinity: only other bureaucracies can satisfy the input needs of a news bureaucracy.”

Why it matters: Why the White House, the Pentagon, and city hall are the permanent news beats: newsrooms are bureaucracies that can only feed on other bureaucracies.

When mapping why some voices are always quoted and others never are.

“Flak” refers to negative responses to a media statement or program. It may take the form of letters, telegrams, phone calls, petitions, lawsuits, speeches and bills before Congress, and other modes of complaint, threat, and punitive action. If certain kinds of fact, position, or program are thought likely to elicit flak, this prospect can be a deterrent.

Why it matters: The fourth filter: punishment doesn't have to arrive — the anticipation of it is enough to kill a story.

When a story dies quietly and nobody gave an order.

This ideology helps mobilize the populace against an enemy, and because the concept is fuzzy it can be used against anybody advocating policies that threaten property interests or support accommodation with Communist states and radicalism.

Why it matters: The fifth filter's genius: a fuzzy enemy label that can be aimed at anyone who threatens property interests.

When a vague ideological label gets deployed against a domestic policy.

The mass media never explain why Andrei Sakharov is worthy and José Luis Massera, in Uruguay, is unworthy—the attention and general dichotomization occur “naturally” as a result of the working of the filters, but the result is the same as if a commissar had instructed the media: “Concentrate on the victims of enemy powers and forget about the victims of friends.”

Why it matters: The book's most quoted line: dichotomization happens naturally, but lands exactly where a censor's order would.

When coverage of foreign victims tracks official enemies and friends.

A propaganda system will consistently portray people abused in enemy states as worthy victims, whereas those treated with equal or greater severity by its own government or clients will be unworthy.

Why it matters: The paired-comparison method of the whole book: worth is assigned by politics, read off the volume and tone of coverage.

When comparing how two similar atrocities get covered.

The victims of Pol Pot, a Communist leader, were worthy, although after he was ousted by the Vietnamese in 1978, Cambodians ceased to be worthy, as U.S. policy shifted toward support of Pol Pot in exile.

Why it matters: The same dead, worthy then unworthy: victimhood tracks U.S. policy shifts, not the scale of suffering.

When a victim group suddenly drops out of the news.

The United States organized what have been called “demonstration elections” in its client states, defined as those whose primary function is to convince the home population that the intervention is well intentioned, that the populace of the invaded and occupied country welcomes the intrusion, and that they are being given a democratic choice.

Why it matters: Elections as stagecraft for the home audience: El Salvador's vote legitimized, Nicaragua's delegitimized, by the same press.

When a foreign election is sold as proof of democracy.

We do not use any kind of “conspiracy” hypothesis to explain massmedia performance. In fact, our treatment is much closer to a “free market” analysis, with the results largely an outcome of the workings of market forces. Most biased choices in the media arise from the preselection of right-thinking people, internalized preconceptions, and the adaptation of personnel to the constraints of ownership, organization, market, and political power. Censorship is largely self-censorship, by reporters and commentators who adjust to the realities of source and media organizational requirements.

Why it matters: The authors' preemptive defense: this is market analysis, not conspiracy — bias comes from hiring, habit, and self-censorship.

When the model gets dismissed as a conspiracy theory.

The special importance of propaganda in what Walter Lippmann referred to as the “manufacture of consent” has long been recognized by writers on public opinion, propaganda, and the political requirements of social order. The powerful are able to fix the premises of discourse, to decide what the general populace is allowed to see, hear, and think about, and to “manage” public opinion by regular propaganda campaigns.

Why it matters: The title's thesis: power doesn't need to win arguments — it sets what arguments are allowed to happen.
