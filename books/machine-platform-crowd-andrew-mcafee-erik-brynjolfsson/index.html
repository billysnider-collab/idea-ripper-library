Machine, Platform, Crowd

by Andrew McAfee & Erik Brynjolfsson

16 cards

Business rebalancing — McAfee & Brynjolfsson's 2017 triple-revolution framework: mind to machine, product to platform, core to crowd; when algorithms beat experts, why platforms win the match game, and why companies survive the crowd anyway.

Card #c50Synthesis

The three rebalancings

We will try to convince you that because of recent technological changes, companies need to rethink the balance between minds and machines, between products and platforms, and between the core and the crowd. The second element in each pair has become much more capable and more powerful just within the past few years, so it needs to be considered with fresh eyes.

— In our words · Source: Machine, Platform, Crowd (Andrew McAfee & Erik Brynjolfsson)

[Use when]

When you're deciding where the next dollar of investment goes: mind or machine, product or platform, core or crowd.

[Why it matters]

The book's entire thesis in one paragraph: nothing is obsolete, but the balance point in all three pairs has moved — and most companies are still calibrated to the old one.

Card #c51Synthesis

136 studies: the formulas beat the experts

They found 136 such studies, covering everything from prediction of IQ to diagnosis of heart disease. In 48% of them, there was no significant difference between the two; the experts, in other words, were on average no better than the formulas. A much bigger blow to the notion of human superiority in judgment came from the finding that in 46% of the studies considered, the human experts actually performed significantly worse than the numbers and formulas alone.

— In our words · Source: Machine, Platform, Crowd (Andrew McAfee & Erik Brynjolfsson)

[Use when]

When an expert's gut says one thing and the model says another.

[Why it matters]

Fifty years of head-to-head research, quantified: experts tie the formula half the time and lose to it nearly half the time — the empirical foundation for handing decisions to algorithms.

Card #c52Synthesis

Meehl: no controversy points so uniformly one way

There is no controversy in social science that shows such a large body of qualitatively diverse studies coming out so uniformly in the same direction as this one [the relative validity of statistical versus clinical prediction]. When you are pushing over 100 investigations, predicting everything from the outcome of football games to the diagnosis of liver disease and when you can hardly come up with a half dozen studies showing even a weak tendency in favor of the clinician, it is time to draw a practical conclusion.

— In our words · Source: Machine, Platform, Crowd (Andrew McAfee & Erik Brynjolfsson)

[Use when]

When someone defends expert intuition against the data.

[Why it matters]

Meehl's summary is the kill shot for expert judgment: across a hundred-plus investigations, the evidence points one direction — use the formula.

Card #c53Synthesis

Tetlock: humanity barely bests a chimp

In a test involving more than 82,000 forecasts, Tetlock found that “humanity barely bests [a] chimp” throwing darts at the possible outcomes.

— In our words · Source: Machine, Platform, Crowd (Andrew McAfee & Erik Brynjolfsson)

[Use when]

When a forecast is presented as expertise rather than a coin flip with footnotes.

[Why it matters]

82,000 expert forecasts, and the species barely beats random darts — the prediction half of the book's case against the expert core.

Card #c54Synthesis

HiPPO: the highest-paid person's opinion

In 2006, Avinash Kaushik and Ronny Kohavi, two data analysis professionals who were then working at Intuit and Microsoft, respectively, came up with the acronym HiPPO to summarize the dominant decision-making style at most companies. It stands for “highest-paid person’s opinion.”

— In our words · Source: Machine, Platform, Crowd (Andrew McAfee & Erik Brynjolfsson)

[Use when]

When a meeting is about to be decided by rank instead of evidence.

[Why it matters]

The name for the old decision regime: whoever is paid most decides most — the exact habit the data-driven rebalancing is meant to kill.

Drivers of the robotic Cambrian Explosion include data, algorithms, networks, the cloud, and exponential improvements in hardware: DANCE. Robots and their kin will be increasingly used wherever work is dull, dirty, dangerous, and dear.

Demand-side economies of scale

Networked goods become more valuable as more people use them. The result is “demand-side economies of scale,” giving an advantage to bigger networks.

— In our words · Source: Machine, Platform, Crowd (Andrew McAfee & Erik Brynjolfsson)

[Use when]

[Why it matters]

The flip of industrial economics: advantage goes to the biggest network, not the biggest factory — which is why platforms tip to winner-take-most.

Why companies survive: incomplete contracting

Companies and other nonmarket organizations are needed to deal with the problem of incomplete contracting—the fact that contracts cannot realistically specify all possible contingencies in the messy real world.
