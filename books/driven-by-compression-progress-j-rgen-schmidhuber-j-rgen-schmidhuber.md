## Steal

This drive maximizes interestingness, the first derivative of subjective beauty or compressibility, that is, the steepness of the learning curve.

## Why it matters

The paper's master equation. Interestingness is not a property of data — it is the rate of change of your compression of it. That reframes boredom, taste, and novelty as facts about the observer's trajectory, not about the object.

## Use when

Use when judging whether something is boring: ask whether the data went flat, or whether your learning curve did.

## Steal

I argue that data becomes temporarily interesting by itself to some self-improving, but computationally limited, subjective observer once he learns to predict or compress the data in a better way, thus making it subjectively simpler and more beautiful.

## Why it matters

The thesis in one sentence: interest is an event inside the observer at the moment of compression progress — not a quality sitting inside the data.

## Use when

Use when the same thing bores you now that electrified you a year ago: you compressed it, and the derivative went to zero.

## Steal

Curiosity is the desire to create or discover more non-random, nonarbitrary, regular data that is novel and surprising not in the traditional sense of Boltzmann and Shannon but in the sense that it allows for compression progress because its regularity was not yet known.

## Why it matters

Kills the Shannon definition of surprise — mere unpredictability — and replaces it with learnability. Surprise that teaches is the only surprise that counts.

## Use when

Use when designing anything meant to hold attention: the target is learnable regularity, not noise.

## Steal

What’s beautiful is not necessarily interesting. A beautiful thing is interesting only as long as it is new, that is, as long as the algorithmic regularity that makes it simple has not yet been fully assimilated by the adaptive observer who is still learning to compress the data better.

## Why it matters

Beauty is a level; interestingness is a slope. A fugue stays beautiful after you have fully compressed it — it just stops being interesting.

## Use when

Use when separating 'this is good' from 'this still has something to teach me.'

## Steal

There is one thing that is involved in all actions and sensory inputs of the agent, namely, the agent itself. To efficiently encode the entire data history, it will profit from creating some sort of internal symbol or code (e. g., a neural activity pattern) representing the agent itself. Whenever this representation is actively used, say, by activating the corresponding neurons through new incoming sensory inputs or otherwise, the agent could be called self-aware or conscious.

## Why it matters

Demystifies consciousness into an engineering move: the self is the most-used symbol in the agent's data history, so the compressor invents it. No mystic value attached.

## Use when

Use when anyone treats self-awareness as magic rather than as the cheapest code for the most recurring pattern in the data.

An unusually large compression breakthrough deserves the name discovery.

## Why it matters

Discovery is not a feeling — it is a measured event: a sudden large drop in the number of bits needed to encode everything you knew. Newton's law is the paper's example: a short piece of code that compresses every falling apple ever seen.

## Steal

one should not focus on the errors of the predictor, but on its improvements.

## Why it matters

The 1991 fix to the 1990 curiosity model: rewarding raw prediction error sends the agent to stare at noise forever, because noise always gives high error. Reward the derivative — the improvement — and the agent hunts learnable regularity instead.

## Use when
