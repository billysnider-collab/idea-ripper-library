How to Take Smart Notes

by Sönke Ahrens

25 rips · Making things · Seeing clearly · Stuck or burned out

Writing and thinking — Sönke Ahrens on Niklas Luhmann's slip-box: how one sociologist turned index cards into 58 books, and how to copy the system.

Mechanisms (7)

mechanism #1208

Number the notes, not the topics

Luhmann filed each new note directly behind the note it answered, so 22 became 22a and then 22a1.

"The trick is that he did not organise his notes by topic, but in the rather abstract way of giving them fixed numbers. The numbers bore no meaning and were only there to identify each note permanently."

Why it matters

Luhmann filed each new note directly behind the note it answered, so 22 became 22a and then 22a1. A topic folder forces you to decide in advance what an idea belongs to. A fixed number lets an idea grow wherever it connects.

Use when: Your files or notes are sorted into categories that no longer match how you actually think about the work.

mechanism #1215

Hunt for the two ideas you believe that can't both be true

Laying notes next to each other surfaces contradictions that stay hidden when each idea lives in its own context.

Laying notes next to each other surfaces contradictions that stay hidden when each idea lives in its own context. Ahrens cites Albert Rothenberg's finding that building oppositions is a reliable way to produce new ideas.

mechanism #1228

Pretend it's just notes for yourself

Slavoj Žižek said this in an interview about how he starts.

"…he wouldn’t be able to write a single sentence if he didn’t start by convincing himself he was only writing down some ideas for himself, and that maybe he could turn it into something publishable later."

Why it matters

Slavoj Žižek said this in an interview about how he starts. Lowering the stakes in your own head gets the words moving. By the time he stopped, the only thing left was revising the draft he already had.

Use when: The importance of a piece of writing is keeping you from starting it.

mechanism #1229

Cut to a second file you'll never open

Deleting a good passage feels like a loss, so people keep material that has no job in the argument.

"For every document I write, I have another called “xy-rest.doc,” and every single time I cut something, I copy it into the other document, convincing myself that I will later look through it and add it back where it might fit. Of course, it never happens – but it still works."

Why it matters

Deleting a good passage feels like a loss, so people keep material that has no job in the argument. Moving it to a holding file removes the loss and the passage disappears from the draft. Ahrens notes Richard Thaler does the same.

Use when: A draft, deck or product is bloated with pieces you can't bring yourself to cut.

Arguments (5)

argument #1213

Brainstorming is a memory test in disguise

Ahrens opens his case against brainstorming with Munger's line.

"Remember the lesson: ‘An idea or a fact is not worth more merely because it is easily available to you.’"

Why it matters

Ahrens opens his case against brainstorming with Munger's line. What comes to mind in a session is what is most recent or vivid, which is a poor proxy for what is most promising. An external store of notes widens the pool.

Use when: A team is about to pick a direction from whatever ideas surface in a single meeting.

argument #1219

Separate writing from proofreading

Finding words needs loose, floating attention.

"We tend to call extremely slow writers, who always try to write as if for print, perfectionists." "A real professional would wait until it was time for proofreading, so he or she can focus on one thing at a time."

Why it matters

Finding words needs loose, floating attention. Correcting them needs narrow, focused attention. Trying to do both in the same pass stalls the work, which is what gets mislabeled as perfectionism.

Use when: You or someone on your team edits every sentence while drafting and the draft barely moves.

Concepts (1)

concept #1221

Rereading feels like learning

Familiarity produces the sensation of knowing without the substance.

"Rereading is especially dangerous because of the mere-exposure effect: The moment we become familiar with something, we start believing we also understand it."

Why it matters

Familiarity produces the sensation of knowing without the substance. Ahrens recommends testing yourself and writing the idea out in your own words, both harder and both more accurate signals of what you've learned.

Use when: You're preparing for a test, a pitch or a board meeting by going over the same material again.

passage #1226

Do only what is easy

Luhmann's statement sounds lazy until you see the system behind it.

"I only do what is easy. I only write when I immediately know how to do it. If I falter for a moment, I put the matter aside and do something else."

Why it matters

Luhmann's statement sounds lazy until you see the system behind it. With dozens of open threads in the slip-box, there was always another task he knew how to do, so getting stuck on one never stopped the work.

Use when: You're grinding on one blocked task and could be moving another forward.

Rules (6)

rule #1209

One idea per note, one screen per note

Luhmann used A6 slips.

"By restricting ourselves to one format, we also restrict ourselves to just one idea per note and force ourselves to be as precise and brief as possible." "A good rule of thumb for working digitally is: Each note should fit onto the screen and there should be no need of scrolling."

Why it matters

Luhmann used A6 slips. The size limit made every note a single unit that could be moved and recombined with others later. A note holding three ideas can only ever sit in one place.

Use when: You're building a library of ideas, research or reusable material and want the pieces to combine later.

rule #1216

Darwin's golden rule: write down the objection at once

Memory filters out what contradicts us.

"…whenever a published fact, a new observation or thought came across me, which was opposed to my general results, to make a memorandum of it without fail and at once; for I had found by experience that such facts and thoughts were far more apt to escape from the memory than favorable ones."

Why it matters

Memory filters out what contradicts us. Darwin countered it by recording disconfirming facts immediately, and wrote that as a result very few objections were raised against his views that he hadn't already noticed and tried to answer.

Use when: You're building a case for a plan or a thesis and want the objections in hand before anyone else raises them.

connection #1224

Compound interest versus a piggy bank

Trollope wrote 250 words every 15 minutes before work, and the pages added up linearly.

"But the main reason is the slip-box, which compares to Trollope’s technique as investing with compounded interest compares to a piggy-bank."

Why it matters

Trollope wrote 250 words every 15 minutes before work, and the pages added up linearly. Luhmann's notes linked to each other, so each new note made the old ones more useful. He published 58 books and hundreds of articles.
