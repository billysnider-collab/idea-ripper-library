The History of Money

David McWilliams · Nonfiction · 5 rips

Money as social tech — from grain and salt to empire and trust.

A specific amount of grain corresponded to something else, such as a day’s work for a laborer, establishing a relationship between the price of food and the price of everything.

Why it matters: Money starts as a conversion table between calories and labor — prices are ratios before they are coins.

Use when explaining why 'real' value talk always smuggles a subsistence unit.

The modern word “salary” comes from this idea of being paid in salt or paid with a coin linked to salt, as too does the expression “worth his salt.” Vespasian meant business.

Why it matters: Etymology is policy fossil: being 'worth your salt' is wage history wearing a proverb costume.

Use when a wage metaphor needs its Roman logistics back.

Can you imagine how much trust in money there must be in a society for people to finance a loan that they know is never actually going to be repaid and yet consider this to be a prudent form of saving?

Why it matters: Credit culture can treat known non-repayment as normal — trust is not optimism, it is a shared fiction with rules.

Use when a 'crazy' debt instrument is actually a trust ritual.

Just as the expansion of empire took time, the disintegration happened gradually, but could the degeneration have been triggered by hyperinflation and the destruction of the credit system?

Why it matters: Monetary collapse is offered as a possible trigger for imperial disintegration, not a sideshow.

Use when decline narratives skip the currency death spiral.

You must be some almighty class of scoundrel if Napoleon describes you as “a pile of shit in silk stockings.” Can you imagine the number of people who must have crossed Bonaparte?

Why it matters: Character assassination from the top of the power stack is still a pricing of reputation in vulgar units.

Use when you need a brutal historical insult that doubles as status economics.
