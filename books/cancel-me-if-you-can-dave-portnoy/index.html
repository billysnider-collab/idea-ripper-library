Cancel Me If You Can

by Dave Portnoy

22 rips · Negotiating · Starting something · Hiring and leading

Building a media company — Dave Portnoy on taking Barstool Sports from a free Boston newspaper to a $650 million sale and back again for one dollar: finding talent, selling ads, doing deals and refusing to back down.

Mechanisms (7)

mechanism #1344

Know your weekly nut

Portnoy moved home, cut every other expense and reduced the business to one number.

"At this point the business model was pretty simple: if I could sell $3,400 worth of ads in the newspaper, I would break even."

Why it matters

Portnoy moved home, cut every other expense and reduced the business to one number. Every week was a fight to hit it. A single break-even target tells you exactly how much selling the week requires.

Use when: You're running a new venture on thin cash and the finances feel vague.

Arguments (1)

argument #1350

Rising nobodies beat established stars

Portnoy paid an unknown $20,000 for an entire spring tour and had a great time.

"In twenty years of doing this shit, I’ve learned that it is far easier to work with nobodies or people who are on their way up than established stars." "But the more you pay people, the more they feel that they’re doing you some kind of favor."

Why it matters

Portnoy paid an unknown $20,000 for an entire spring tour and had a great time. On the next tour he paid established acts over $100,000 a show, and they arrived late and didn't care. The money bought less commitment.

Use when: You're deciding between a proven big name and a hungry up-and-comer.

concept #1357

Direct visitors are worth more than clicks

Portnoy turned down a link-sharing program that would have padded traffic.

"They were direct, intentional visitors who knew where they were going and what they wanted to find."

Why it matters

Portnoy turned down a link-sharing program that would have padded traffic. Drive-by clicks didn't buy T-shirts. People who came on purpose bought merch, commented and stayed, and that was what investors paid for.

Use when: You're tempted to inflate traffic or user numbers with visitors who don't care about you.

evidence #1358

Take the deal when the timing and the partner are right

Portnoy expected the talks to end at $7.5 million.

"The opening bid from Peter Chernin and Mike Kerns was $7.5 million for 51 percent of the company, and I would retain all creative control over the content. It wasn’t just low; it was embarrassingly low." "The timing was right and the number seemed right. It wasn’t even close to what the company was actually worth, but it all worked out in the end."

Why it matters

Portnoy expected the talks to end at $7.5 million. Chernin came back at $12.5 million. He took it for $5 million of personal security, an end to his personal liability, equity his partners could cash in and an investor he trusted. Five years later the company sold at a $650 million valuation.

Use when: An offer undervalues you but solves your real constraints and comes from a partner you trust.

evidence #1365

Buy it back for a dollar

Penn needed a money-losing asset gone to pursue its ESPN deal.

"At the time, Barstool was losing close to $12 million a year. I would buy the entire company back for one dollar, taking us off Penn’s books and inheriting all the expenses."

Why it matters

Penn needed a money-losing asset gone to pursue its ESPN deal. Portnoy offered to take on the losses in exchange for ownership, plus a ten-year promise not to disparage ESPN Bet. A buyer's need to unload can be worth more than the price.

Use when: The owner of something you want is under pressure to get rid of it.

passage #1347

The job ad that filters for love of the game

Only a few people answered.

"Barstool is looking to hire writers. We don’t pay at all, but we make up for it by offering zero benefits."

Why it matters

Only a few people answered. Three of them became the original "Core 4" with Portnoy, and one still writes for Barstool two decades later. A blunt ad screens out everyone except the people who would do the work anyway.

Use when: You can't pay yet and need contributors who care about the work itself.

rule #1348

Hire for a track record of output

Big Cat was a caller to Portnoy's radio show who already ran his own Chicago blog.

"I needed to see that prospective hires were consistent over time; that they had been updating and adding to their own blog at a pace and frequency that were within the realm of how we did things at Barstool."

Why it matters

Big Cat was a caller to Portnoy's radio show who already ran his own Chicago blog. Portnoy knew within seconds that he was the hire. An existing body of work at your pace beats any interview.

Use when: You're hiring for a role where sustained output matters and candidates can show what they've already made.

rule #1355

Don't beg for investment

Portnoy wasn't looking for money when the Chernin Group approached.

"If you ever have to beg for an investment, if you feel like a used car salesman during the process, if you aren’t 100 percent comfortable saying yes to a deal, then don’t do it. Investment should never be a last resort or come out of desperation."

Why it matters

Portnoy wasn't looking for money when the Chernin Group approached. He gave them access to his analytics and nothing else. Being able to walk away is what let the price rise.

Use when: You're raising money and the process feels like pleading.

rule #1360

Your investors are business partners

This is the reference Portnoy gives founders.

"The Chernin guys did what they said. They gave us money. They gave us credibility. They stayed out of our way. But they will run you over if they think it’s in their best interests."

Why it matters

This is the reference Portnoy gives founders. Chernin turned a $12 million stake into about $250 million and never offered him a raise or held a celebration. Expecting loyalty would have turned a good deal into a grievance.

Use when: You're taking money from investors and want to know what to expect from them.

rule #1361

Pay before they ask

Portnoy saw the other side of this with Chernin, who never offered him more.

"If I think an employee of mine deserves a raise, I give it to them often before they ask for it." "The way I do things is if you’re killing it, I renegotiate and pay you more."

Why it matters

Portnoy saw the other side of this with Chernin, who never offered him more. He was ready to walk away from his own company. Unprompted raises keep strong performers from shopping themselves around.

Use when: Someone on your team is clearly outperforming their deal and hasn't brought it up.

His sales team had agreed to lease the One Bite name to a pizza maker for ad dollars.

"Why would I give the IP to somebody else? If I was going to make a frozen pizza, I wanted to own the pizza and the name and not let somebody else get rich off my IP."

Why it matters

His sales team had agreed to lease the One Bite name to a pizza maker for ad dollars. Portnoy stopped the deal and restructured it so Barstool owned the product. He then had to admit on camera that the first version tasted bad.
