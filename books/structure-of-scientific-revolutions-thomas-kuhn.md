## Steal

A paradigm is not a big idea or a theory; it's a shared example of what a solved problem looks like — an accepted instance of actual practice (including its tools, methods, and standards) that a group uses as the template for its next problem. The mechanism: a group can only do work as a group once it tacitly agrees on what counts as a good piece of work — and that agreement is carried by concrete exemplars, not by stated rules.

## Why it matters

Kuhn's move was to locate what binds a community not in its theories but in its exemplars — 'accepted examples of actual scientific practice... which include law, theory, application, and instrumentation together.' A novice doesn't learn the paradigm by memorizing rules; they learn by working through the model problems the community treats as exemplary. That is why paradigms are tacit, and why they are nearly impossible to state from inside.

## Use when

Use when diagnosing a company's internal culture: the paradigm is the exemplar of 'a good quarter / a good hire / a good design' that everyone tacitly references. Use when you can't articulate a personal belief — look for the concrete example you're actually imitating, not the principle you say you hold.

## Steal

The bulk of work in any mature field is not testing the framework — it's working out the details the framework already implies. Kuhn calls it 'mop-up work.' The framework is ASSUMED; only the puzzles are solved. Most 'productive work' is elaboration and confirmation, not questioning. You are not testing whether the system is right; you are extending how far it reaches.

## Why it matters

'Normal science' names the mode where the paradigm is not on trial — 'a highly cumulative enterprise... the steady extension of the scope and precision of scientific knowledge.' In normal mode no one asks 'is this paradigm true?'; they ask 'what does this paradigm imply here, and can I make it work?' The paradigm supplies both the problem and the standard for a valid solution.

## Use when

Use when auditing a team or an org: most of what it does is mop-up — confirming and elaborating the existing playbook, not testing it. Use to name the two modes honestly: 'normal' (execute the framework) vs 'extraordinary' (question the framework) — and to see that most people, most of the time, are in normal mode and shouldn't pretend otherwise.

## Steal

A contradiction does not immediately overturn a framework. First it registers as an 'anomaly' — a violation of expectation — and the framework absorbs it: people work around it, ignore it, or reclassify it. Frameworks don't die from one bad result; they die from anomalies that accumulate and keep resisting being made to 'conform.'

## Why it matters

Kuhn's anomaly is the failure of an expected outcome, not a refutation: 'anomalies, or violations of expectation.' The key dynamic is that the framework protects itself first — 'normal science often suppresses fundamental novelties because they are necessarily subversive of its basic commitments.' But because no framework is perfectly arbitrary, the suppression can't hold forever.

## Use when

Use when your team keeps hitting a result that contradicts the plan but everyone rationalizes it away — name it an anomaly and track it instead of dismissing it. Use to predict which 'small inconsistencies' in a company, a relationship, or a belief will eventually break it: the ones that recur and can't be made to fit.

## Steal

A framework doesn't collapse cleanly — it enters 'crisis,' and the tell is behavioral, not a single decisive event. The rules loosen, people will 'try anything,' explicit discontent surfaces, and the field splinters into competing versions of itself. You diagnose crisis by its symptoms: first-principles arguments, contradictory 'fixes,' and open complaint.

## Why it matters

Kuhn lists the symptoms of the transition from normal to extraordinary research: 'the proliferation of competing articulations, the willingness to try anything, the expression of explicit discontent, the recourse to philosophy and to debate over fundamentals.' And separately: 'proliferation of versions of a theory is a very usual symptom of crisis.' The framework in crisis loses its grip on what counts as a valid move, so everything becomes a candidate move.

## Use when

Use to read a company or movement in trouble: if everyone is suddenly debating first principles, proposing contradictory 'fixes,' and openly complaining, you are in crisis — the old way has lost its grip. Use to distinguish 'in crisis' from 'hard times': crisis means the shared framework itself is being questioned, not just the results.

## Steal

The switch from one framework to another is not additive — you don't build the new on top of the old. It's a discontinuous leap: 'far from a cumulative process.' After the switch, the old framework isn't a special case of the new one; it's simply left behind. Change here is conversion, not accumulation — and the difference matters, because a conversion can't be achieved by piling up more of the old.

## Why it matters

Kuhn's central attack on the textbook image of progress: the transition to a new paradigm is 'far from a cumulative process, one achieved by an articulation or extension of the old.' Real revolutions don't extend the old view — they replace it. That is what makes them 'revolutions' rather than improvements.

## Use when

Use when a team or person keeps trying to 'extend' a broken mental model with patches instead of switching — the tell that genuine change is required is that extension no longer works. Use to separate iterating (accumulation) from changing your mind (conversion), and to stop treating a conversion as if it were just a large iteration.

## Steal

Two frameworks can't be fully judged against each other by a shared, neutral standard — because the standard itself lives inside one of them. 'Incommensurable' means the successor is 'not only incompatible but often actually incommensurable with that which has gone before.' You cannot referee the dispute from outside both frameworks, because there is no outside.

## Why it matters

Kuhn's hardest claim: after a revolution, the new tradition and the old one don't just disagree — they can't be compared point-by-point, because what even counts as a valid problem, a valid solution, or a valid standard has changed. There is no neutral observation language both sides accept, so 'who's right' can't be settled with a shared ruler.

## Use when

Use when two sides of an argument each look obviously right to themselves and obviously wrong to the other — check whether they share a definition of 'what would count as proof' at all. If not, you're in an incommensurability situation, and arguing facts is pointless until you argue the yardstick. Use to spot when a new strategy can't be evaluated by the old one's metrics.

## Steal

After you adopt a new framework, the SAME inputs read differently. It's like the gestalt figure — 'the marks on paper that were first seen as a bird are now seen as an antelope.' The change isn't in the data; it's in what the data shows. Afterward, the person 'works in a different world' — so you can't convert someone by adding data, only by getting them to see differently.

## Why it matters

Kuhn uses the gestalt image for what a paradigm shift feels like: the facts don't change, the seeing does. 'After discovering oxygen Lavoisier worked in a different world'; 'after Copernicus, astronomers lived in a different world.' It's why revolutions can't be forced by argument alone — the other side isn't refusing your data, they're literally not seeing what you see.

## Use when

Use when someone 'just doesn't get it' despite having all the same information — the problem may be a gestalt gap, not a knowledge gap, and no amount of added data fixes a difference in what the data means. Use to recognize the moment your own view flipped: it felt like the world rearranged itself, not like you learned a new fact.

## Steal

After a framework wins, the community rewrites its own history to make the winner look inevitable and the losers look naive. The record-keeping documents 'inevitably disguise not only the role but the very existence of the revolutions that produced them.' The path that was actually contingent and contested gets edited into a straight line of progress.

## Why it matters

Kuhn's 'invisibility of revolutions': the account of how the change actually happened is systematically erased by the very documents that teach the next generation. The rewritten history makes the new framework look like the obvious, cumulative outcome — so each generation is taught a version of its field with the revolutions hidden from it.

## Use when

Use when reading a company's founding story, a movement's official history, or your own past decisions — ask what got edited out to make the outcome look inevitable. Use to treat any 'how we got here' narrative as already-rewritten: the contingency and the rejected alternatives are precisely what the telling erased.

## Steal

A mature framework is conservative BY DESIGN: it 'often suppresses fundamental novelties because they are necessarily subversive of its basic commitments.' The resistance isn't stupidity or bad faith — it's the framework doing its job. Novelty threatens the shared order that makes normal work possible, so normal work suppresses it. The people most invested in the current way are, structurally, the last to see the new thing that will replace it.

## Why it matters

Kuhn explains why fields resist the new: the paradigm's 'basic commitments' are what make puzzle-solving possible at all, and a fundamental novelty undermines exactly those commitments. So resistance to novelty is structural, not personal — which means a field can be healthy and simultaneously resistant to the very discovery that will eventually replace it.

## Use when

Use when an org, a guild, or your own habits resist a good new idea — before calling it 'politics' or 'ego,' ask whether the idea threatens the shared assumptions that make the current work function. Use to predict who will be last to accept a coming change: the people most fluent in the current framework.

## Steal

'Progress' is measured by the framework's own internal standard of good puzzle-solving, not by any external approach to truth. 'We may... have to relinquish the notion... that changes of paradigm carry scientists and those who learn from them closer and closer to the truth.' A framework can be getting objectively better at solving its own problems while drifting from the thing it claims to be about.

## Why it matters

Kuhn's most consequential and most misread claim: the progress of science is real, but it is progress in the paradigm's own terms — better puzzle-solving — not convergence on a final truth. This separates 'the field is improving' (true, by its own standards) from 'the field is approaching reality' (not something the framework can guarantee).

## Use when

Use when 'we're making progress' is used to mean 'we're getting closer to being right' — ask: progress by whose yardstick, toward what? Use to evaluate a company, a career, or a belief system: it can be getting better at its own game while drifting from the thing it was originally about.
