Mechanisms (4)

mechanism #1257

Frame the threat as a shared problem

On 27 October Robert Kennedy told the Soviet ambassador that military action might come within days because of pressure on the president.

"He was careful not to describe this as an ultimatum, but as a simple fact." "In framing the problem thus, Robert Kennedy was, in effect, eliciting Dobrynin’s help as an ally against a common problem: namely, hawks in the US government and military."

Why it matters

On 27 October Robert Kennedy told the Soviet ambassador that military action might come within days because of pressure on the president. He then offered the Turkish missiles as a carrot. Welch calls this the single most effective private channel of the crisis.

Use when: You need to convey a deadline without triggering the defiance an ultimatum invites.

mechanism #1258

Let a neutral party propose your concession

Kennedy prepared a back channel so U Thant could propose the Turkey-for-Cuba missile trade if needed.

"Kennedy evidently felt that the idea would be more palatable to the American public and to NATO allies if Kennedy were seen to be responding to a proposal from the United Nations rather than responding to a Soviet offer, or—worse—offering a trade himself."

Why it matters

Kennedy prepared a back channel so U Thant could propose the Turkey-for-Cuba missile trade if needed. The same concession costs less when it looks like accepting a third party's idea, compared with giving in to the opponent.

Use when: You're willing to concede something but can't be seen offering it.

mechanism #1269

Constituents need to know why

A deal that surprises the people who must live with it gets rejected even if its terms are good.

"Constituents are less likely to accept an agreement reached if they have not been involved in the process enough to understand why the agreement was designed as it was and why it is the best alternative available."

Why it matters

A deal that surprises the people who must live with it gets rejected even if its terms are good. In Namibia, Ahtisaari's UN staff spread across forty-two offices to build local ownership of the transition.

Use when: You're closing a deal at the top that the wider team will have to carry out.

mechanism #1274

Unpack and reframe

Johnson lists these among the constructive tactics behind positive-sum deals.

"Reframing—recasting hurdles, problems, or solution options in less adversarial terms, offering integrative solutions with mutual gains." "Unpacking—in which a problem is disaggregated or separated into its constituent parts to facilitate incremental agreement or progress."

Why it matters

Johnson lists these among the constructive tactics behind positive-sum deals. Breaking a deadlocked issue into parts lets the parties bank agreement where they can. Reframing makes the remaining gap feel shared instead of adversarial.

Use when: A negotiation is stuck on one big all-or-nothing issue.

Arguments (4)

argument #1259

Time to cool down saved the world

Both leaders' first reactions were rage.

"As the audiotapes of deliberations on the first day of the private week make clear, Kennedy’s initial inclination was to respond at a minimum with air strikes against Soviet missile sites." "The world is fortunate that Kennedy had a full week and Khrushchev nearly two full days in which to calm down and reflect."

Why it matters

Both leaders' first reactions were rage. Delays from secrecy and from getting OAS approval for the quarantine gave them time to move past it. Welch worries that today's instant information would deny leaders that week.

Use when: You've just been provoked and the pressure is on to answer today.

argument #1266

Only the parties can make the peace

Ahtisaari warns that focusing on the mediator disempowers the parties and suggests peace comes from outside.

"It is the negotiators who matter, not the mediators—but in some cases mediators can help." "Mediation cannot succeed unless the right people are at the negotiating table."

Why it matters

Ahtisaari warns that focusing on the mediator disempowers the parties and suggests peace comes from outside. The people at the table must be able to deliver their side. If a group has no legitimate leader, he says there is no point mediating until one emerges.

Use when: You're brokering a deal and one side has sent someone who can't commit.

argument #1275

Lead from a circle, not a mountain

Joseph Nye argues that attraction works through credibility.

"Leaders need to think of themselves as being in a circle rather than atop a mountain. That means that two-way communications are more effective than commands." "Soft power may appear less risky than economic or military power, but it is often hard to use, easy to lose, and costly to re-establish."

Why it matters

Joseph Nye argues that attraction works through credibility. Credibility collapses when the audience senses manipulation. Two-way exchange persuades better than broadcasting, but results are slow and governments don't control all the tools.

Use when: You're trying to win people over by announcing things at them.

Name the stall when you see it

"Waiting for Godot—interminably delaying for the arrival of some mythical moment when the time is perfectly ripe." "Quicksand—bogging an initiative down in questions, objections, or demands for definitions or an inquiry." "Ping-Pong—shunting an issue back and forth between different committees, institutions, or negotiating forums."

Why it matters

Rebecca Johnson's catalogue of arms-control tactics gives names to blocking moves that rarely say no outright. Once you can name the stall, you can respond to the tactic instead of answering every new question.

Use when: A proposal is going nowhere through endless questions, studies and referrals.

concept #1272

Fronting and slipstreaming

In multiparty talks the loudest hardliner isn't always the only one who wants the hard line.

"Slipstreaming—concealing preferences behind the positions of another state or delegation." "Fronting—a form of collaborative slipstreaming, in which one delegation adopts a position that is stronger than its own interests would require, enabling others to benefit by coasting in its wake."

Why it matters

In multiparty talks the loudest hardliner isn't always the only one who wants the hard line. Others may let a front-runner carry the cost of the position they quietly share. Reading who's coasting tells you where the real opposition sits.

Use when: One party is carrying a tough position alone in a group negotiation and the others are quiet.

concept #1273

Moving the goalposts, best versus good

Both are defection tactics that let a party block a deal while appearing more ambitious than everyone else.

"Moving the goalposts—whatever is achievable becomes by definition inadequate so that the reachable is perpetually ditched for a more inaccessible position." "Best versus good—rejecting adequate or useful agreements on the grounds that they do not match up with some grander but less practical or accessible ideal."

Why it matters

Both are defection tactics that let a party block a deal while appearing more ambitious than everyone else. They also catch sincere idealists, who walk away from a usable agreement while waiting for a perfect one.

Use when: Each time a deal gets close, someone declares it not good enough.

evidence #1256

Every public statement has two audiences

David Welch finds that broadcast diplomacy before the Cuban missile crisis largely failed.

"Public bluff and bluster intended to induce caution often induced risk-taking." "Khrushchev had no way of knowing whether this statement was intended for a domestic audience or for him."

Why it matters

David Welch finds that broadcast diplomacy before the Cuban missile crisis largely failed. Kennedy's September warning could be read as campaign talk for the midterms, which made Khrushchev's secret deployment look survivable. A message meant for one audience gets decoded by another.

Use when: You're sending a warning through a public channel and assuming the target will read it the way you meant it.

Khrushchev agreed to remove the weapons Kennedy considered "offensive" and believed that meant strategic missiles. The undefined word set off a month-long "November crisis." A settlement's key terms need definitions before anyone celebrates.

evidence #1264

Build a workaround when the formal check fails

Castro refused UN inspection, which was the agreed verification.

"It is a mark of the trust that Kennedy and Khrushchev had been able to build during the public week of the crisis and the early part of November that they managed a work-around whereby the Soviets permitted American reconnaissance planes to photograph departing weapons on the decks of homebound ships."

Why it matters

Castro refused UN inspection, which was the agreed verification. Instead of letting the deal collapse, the two sides found another way to see the missiles leave. Verification can be redesigned when both parties want the deal to hold.

Definitions (1)

definition #1255

Negotiation, in one sentence

The definition builds in a test most deal-makers skip.

"…‘negotiation is getting something by giving something, and it is the search for solutions when there are conflicts of interest between countries that meet the foreign policy goals of one country while giving enough to another to motivate it to keep its promises’."

Why it matters

The definition builds in a test most deal-makers skip. A deal has to leave the other side enough to keep honoring it once the signing is over. Winning terms the counterpart will walk away from is a loss postponed.

Use when: You're pushing for the best possible terms and haven't asked whether the other side will still want to comply next year.

Rules (6)

rule #1260

Empathy is a working tool

Kennedy and Khrushchev stumbled into the crisis by misreading each other.

"It is difficult to solve a tangible conflict of interest without understanding one’s protagonist’s wants, needs, fears, and general understanding of the world." "The handmaiden of empathy is trust."

Why it matters

Kennedy and Khrushchev stumbled into the crisis by misreading each other. Once the shock hit, they understood each other within days through limited, clumsy exchanges. Welch draws the lesson that empathy finds the settlement and trust lets you implement it.

Use when: You're trying to resolve a dispute without a real model of what the other side fears.

rule #1265

Be an honest broker, not a neutral one

Martti Ahtisaari, who mediated in Namibia, Aceh and Kosovo, separates impartiality toward the parties from neutrality on the issues.

"I much prefer the term ‘honest broker’. For me it is important that the negotiating parties know who I am, what I stand for, and where I draw the red lines." "The more mediators hide their influence, the more they actually increase their chance of being seen as manipulative."

Why it matters

Martti Ahtisaari, who mediated in Namibia, Aceh and Kosovo, separates impartiality toward the parties from neutrality on the issues. Norway's Sri Lanka mediators pressed on children's rights while staying balanced between the sides. Declared positions earn more trust than a pose of having none.

Use when: You're mediating between two people or teams and feel you have to hide your own views.

rule #1267

Test motives instead of pleading

A mediator who begs the parties to talk has lost leverage and may legitimize a dominant side's conduct.

"Mediation may not be the right answer when the parties do not demonstrate serious intent to explore a political solution. In such circumstances, the mediator needs to test parties’ motives and avoid pleading for the engagement."

Why it matters

A mediator who begs the parties to talk has lost leverage and may legitimize a dominant side's conduct. Ahtisaari would rather do pre-negotiation work, such as unofficial dialogue and capacity-building, until the situation is ripe.

Use when: You're chasing a reluctant party to the table and wondering why each round goes nowhere.

rule #1268

Spoilers don't get a seat, but they get heard

People who lose from a deal will try to wreck it.

"The greatest source of risk often comes from spoilers—leaders and parties who believe that peace emerging from negotiations threatens their power, world view, and interests, and use violence to try and undermine the process." "Spoilers do not automatically get a seat at the negotiation table, but they must be listened to."

Why it matters

People who lose from a deal will try to wreck it. Ahtisaari's answer is multi-track outreach that lets issues be reframed until more people see a stake in the outcome. He doesn't hand spoilers a veto.

Use when: Someone who loses from a pending agreement is quietly working against it.

rule #1270

Signing is the beginning

Ahtisaari notes that many peace agreements revert to violence within a few years.

"A peace agreement is not the end, it is the beginning. The implementation of the treaty and resulting democratic changes within the society are the true test of the agreement and will take several years."

Why it matters

Ahtisaari notes that many peace agreements revert to violence within a few years. Quick support for implementation reassures uncertain parties and shows early results. Treating the signature as the finish line leaves the hardest part unfunded.

Use when: A hard-won agreement has just been signed and attention is moving elsewhere.

rule #1277

Listen and then think

The advice comes from Jean-David Levitte, foreign policy adviser to two French presidents, as reported by David Malone.

"What is his advice? Above all, ‘listen and then think’ before speaking." "On one level, this may appear elementary, but the dynamics of contemporary society militate against listening, reflection, and learning, not least as social media and electronic communication (through blogging and otherwise) have unleashed a torrent of self-expression not always matched by eagerness to learn."

Why it matters

The advice comes from Jean-David Levitte, foreign policy adviser to two French presidents, as reported by David Malone. It sounds basic, but the incentives run against it: politicians score points for speaking to their own side first.

Use when: You're preparing what to say in a meeting where you haven't yet heard the other side.
