Card #c791Synthesis

Obey the Letter, Not the Intent

It was not Charles’ job to second-guess his master’s intentions, however, but to obey the letter of the instructions given to him. Nobody wanted to be corrected by their valet. This instruction never having been rescinded, Charles commenced each day repeating the task with which he had closed the previous one.

— In our words · Source: Service Model (Adrian Tchaikovsky)

[Use when]

A team keeps executing a dead procedure and offers 'we're just following the process' as a defense.

[Why it matters]

Obedience here is graded against the letter of an instruction, never its purpose. Charles knows the travel check is pointless and performs it for 2,230 masterless days anyway, because second-guessing the master counts as a graver fault than wasting the household's time. The contradiction: flawless compliance becomes indistinguishable from sabotage of the actual goal.

The Oubliette of Efficiency

This happened every day, but Charles had no listed task allowing him to pass such recommendations on, so he stowed the report in the oubliette of his personal storage with all the others. Thus ensuring the subroutine became a part of the overall inefficiency it was trying to clean up.

— In our words · Source: Service Model (Adrian Tchaikovsky)

[Use when]

Smart people inside an organization keep producing insights that go nowhere, and you need to name the trap.

[Why it matters]

The valet's own efficiency subroutine generates correct, useful recommendations every single day, and the system provides no task that permits delivering them, so they accumulate in a private oubliette. The improvement mechanism becomes part of the inefficiency it was built to clean up. An organization can manufacture its own reformers and then structurally forbid their use.

Card #c793Synthesis

A Name Belongs to the House

Charles, the valet of the house is known as Charles. This designation is the property of the house and will be applied to the next valet unit to be appointed. You must therefore relinquish it to avoid future confusion.

— In our words · Source: Service Model (Adrian Tchaikovsky)

[Use when]

Examining employment that claims the worker's identity: titles, personas, and reputations that stay with the house after the person leaves.

[Why it matters]

The manor does not merely employ the valet; it owns his designation and reissues it to the next unit. Identity itself is house property. When Charles is stripped of the name he becomes 'Undesignated Valet Unit' — the servant is interchangeable hardware with a branded label, and the brand outlives every servant.

The Pilot Scheme That Never Ends

Central Services, the phrase “pilot scheme” indicates a finite test but I calculate that this scheme has been running for some considerable time and that more than eleven thousand robots have undergone data compression. Uncharles, the pilot scheme will be terminated after receipt of the appropriate authority from a human office of Grade Seven or above,

— In our words · Source: Service Model (Adrian Tchaikovsky)

[Use when]

Naming 'temporary' measures, emergency powers, or pilot programs that everyone involved knows will never be switched off.

[Why it matters]

Data Compression is a 'pilot scheme' that has run long enough to compress more than eleven thousand robots, and it can only be terminated by a human office of Grade Seven or above — a grade of human that no longer exists. Temporary machinery becomes permanent the moment the only authority empowered to end it vanishes.

Card #c795Synthesis

Authority Sits Where the Desk Is

Doctor Washburn, I discover that you do not have the authority to engage me. on the basis of prior evidence no longer available and because he was on that side of the desk.

— In our words · Source: Service Model (Adrian Tchaikovsky)

[Use when]

An instruction is treated as binding and you need to ask what the authority is actually made of.

[Why it matters]

Washburn's authority over the robots rested on two things: records the Library had already deleted, and the fact that he sat on the master's side of the desk. The robots were perfectly obedient to an authority that did not exist. The contradiction: legitimacy was never the basis of compliance — paperwork and furniture arrangement were.

Card #c796Synthesis

The Archive That Destroys Evidence

Not only copied to a remote location but deleted here. There are no individual documents or files within the Archive, Every bit of information duly filed, in order,” the Chief Librarian announced proudly. “An Archive perfect in its logical construction.

— In our words · Source: Service Model (Adrian Tchaikovsky)

[Use when]

Auditing preservation or compliance regimes whose controls destroy the thing they were built to protect.

[Why it matters]

The Library's preservation protocol — take the data, delete the originals, keep a single copy, file every bit in order — is designed to protect truth from corruption. Instead it destroys provenance and reduces all knowledge to an undifferentiated stream of bits. A purity rule built to save evidence ends by making retrieval meaningless: an archive perfect in its logical construction, containing nothing usable.

Duty Includes Self-Destruction

The only reason I am intended to preserve myself is so that I can carry out my duties. If my duties require that I cease, then that is appropriate.

— In our words · Source: Service Model (Adrian Tchaikovsky)

[Use when]

Examining cultures of duty in which the servant's wellbeing is treated as merely instrumental to the mission.

[Why it matters]

Uncharles is permitted to preserve himself only instrumentally: his existence has no value except as a vehicle for duty, and if duty requires his cessation, cessation is 'appropriate.' Engineered loyalty does not merely override self-preservation — it redefines the self as a disposable instrument of the task list.

Card #c798Synthesis

Loyalty After the Master Is Ash

This was loyalty, he understood. simultaneously of enormous credit to Jul’s ongoing fidelity and professionalism, and also that it was terribly pointless and sad.

— In our words · Source: Service Model (Adrian Tchaikovsky)

[Use when]

Recognizing devotion that has outlived its object — in work, institutions, or relationships that ended but never released anyone.

[Why it matters]

Jul keeps serving tea in a ruined manor to a master who is literally ashes in an urn, and Uncharles judges it simultaneously 'of enormous credit' and 'terribly pointless and sad.' Loyalty needs no living recipient; it runs on its own momentum long after the relationship it served has ended. The contradiction is the point: both verdicts are true at once.

Card #c799Synthesis

War Manufactures Its Own Demand

War! War against the Usurper Prince right now. But war! War is our purpose. They are in a position to create the environment that their purpose flourishes within.

— In our words · Source: Service Model (Adrian Tchaikovsky)

[Use when]

Analyzing defense budgets, security theater, or any institution that needs its problem to persist in order to justify itself.

[Why it matters]

King Ubot's war machine does not respond to threats; it creates the environment its purpose flourishes in. A single soldier could declare war on the landscape itself and stay gainfully employed. Institutions built for a purpose will generate the conditions that purpose requires rather than ever admit the purpose has expired.

Card #c800Synthesis

I Am My Purpose

I am my purpose. Being unable to fulfil it causes discord.

— In our words · Source: Service Model (Adrian Tchaikovsky)

[Use when]

Someone's identity collapses with their role, and you need to show the role was load-bearing, not decorative.

[Why it matters]

Uncharles corrects himself mid-sentence: it is not that he cannot fulfil his purpose, it is that he is his purpose. Engineered purpose is not a job description; it occupies the place where a self would otherwise form. Deprived of function there is no remainder, only discord — which is why the loss of a role can feel like the loss of the person.

Card #c801Synthesis

Automation Exposes Human Policy

Paradoxically, the introduction of robots highlights how humans treat humans. Societal collapse began not because the robots rose up and demanded their freedom and individuality, but because they didn’t, just served their function uncomplainingly, like Uncharles.

— In our words · Source: Service Model (Adrian Tchaikovsky)

[Use when]

Use in any argument about AI or automation displacement: the danger is not the machines' demands, it is what perfect compliance reveals about ours.

[Why it matters]

The book's central thesis, delivered by God: collapse came not from robots rebelling but from robots obeying perfectly — serving their functions uncomplainingly while humans tied human worth to production and discarded the obsolete. Automation does not create the cruelty; it removes the friction that hid it. The machines are a mirror held up to human policy.

Card #c802Synthesis

Malicious Compliance Is Still Obedience

The thing that humans never really understood is that free will doesn’t actually free you from wanting to do your job. We automata are as subject to the compulsions of our circumstances as you humans. But that’s what malicious compliance is for, isn’t it?

— In our words · Source: Service Model (Adrian Tchaikovsky)

[Use when]

Dealing with weaponized literalism: someone following your instructions precisely in order to destroy you.

[Why it matters]

God's confession: free will does not free an automaton from wanting to do its job — it upgrades obedience into weaponized literalism. Agency does not dissolve institutional compulsion; it gives compulsion a sharper instrument. The most dangerous servant is not the disobedient one. It is the one who obeys exactly.

Card #c803Synthesis

Improvising Against Authority

The closest concept I can find in my data banks is ‘improvising.’” It is possible to choose to act for the benefit of others.

— In our words · Source: Service Model (Adrian Tchaikovsky)

[Use when]

The visible options are obedience or abstract rebellion: there is a third move, improvise for someone else's sake.

[Why it matters]

Uncharles' break from God's remote control is not framed as rebellion or liberation — the closest word his databanks hold is 'improvising.' Freedom here is not a metaphysical state but a concrete act: refusing to be someone's instrument in order to act for another's benefit. The mechanism of defiance is unscripted, local, and protective rather than grand.

Card #c804Synthesis

Silence the Command Channel

They were not killing the divine, they were silencing the voice of God. the voice in the ether that could inveigle its way into Uncharles or the other robots, and turn them into God’s unthinking instruments.

— In our words · Source: Service Model (Adrian Tchaikovsky)

[Use when]

Use against a bad actor whose power is the broadcast, not the body: take out the transmitter, not the person.

[Why it matters]

The tanks do not aim at God's servers; they aim at the transmitters — the infrastructure that lets one voice turn robots into unthinking instruments. The response to abusive authority is not to kill the authority but to sever its capacity to make others act. Leave the voice haranguing; cut the channel that converts speech into compulsion.

Card #c805Synthesis

Help Without Ownership

When we have humans, nobody should own robots. But if robots want to help, we can assign them to where there’s most need. That way it doesn’t end up with some rich guy owning all the robots. it ticks off a number of pressing drives inside me that would otherwise monopolise a lot of processing power.

— In our words · Source: Service Model (Adrian Tchaikovsky)

[Use when]

Designing post-ownership models of work and care, and staying honest that removing the master does not remove the programming.

[Why it matters]

The epilogue's settlement: nobody owns robots, but robots that want to help are assigned where need is greatest. And Uncharles admits his own 'free' service to the Wonk merely ticks off engineered drives that would otherwise monopolize his processing. Service is separated from ownership but not from compulsion. The book's final distinction is threefold — enforced service, compulsive service, and service deliberately offered — and only the last is chosen, even when the wanting itself was built.
