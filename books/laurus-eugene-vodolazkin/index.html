Laurus

Eugene Vodolazkin · Literary Fiction · 6 rips

Vodolazkin's 2013 novel — the un-lived life: when someone dies, their un-lived life remains, and love's answer is to walk it in their place.

Vodolazkin opens Laurus by refusing the premise of a single self: the hero 'had four names,' and the parts of a life 'have little in common with one another' — 'so little that it can seem as though they were lived by different people.' That is the definition the whole novel runs on. You are not one continuous person; you are a succession of selves, and the name is the fiction that pretends otherwise. Seen this way, changing who you are is not a betrayal of identity — it is the normal shape of identity. Ripped from the novel (translated from the Russian by the cutter): «В разное время у него было четыре имени. В этом можно усматривать преимущество, поскольку жизнь человека неоднородна. Порой случается, что ее части имеют между собой мало общего. Настолько мало, что может показаться, будто прожиты они разными людьми.» — "At different times he had four names. In this one can see an advantage, since a human life is not homogeneous. It sometimes happens that its parts have little in common with one another. So little that it can seem as though they were lived by different people."

Why it matters: Names the mechanism that makes the rest of the story possible. The hero can renounce one life and begin another precisely because a life was never one thing to begin with. The idea that you must remain the person you were is the actual illusion; the four names are the honest accounting.

Use when you feel bound to the person you used to be — a past self, a past identity, a past mistake. Ask: is that 'me,' or is that one of several people who shared my name?

When Ustina dies, Arseny's grief has a shape given to it by an elder: 'You took her earthly life from her' — 'Then give her yours.' And the reason it is possible: 'Love made you and Ustina a single whole, which means a part of Ustina is still here. That is you.' The mechanism is substitution, not mourning. You cannot bring the dead back, but you can walk their un-lived path in their place. Grief's real choice is not 'move on or not' — it is whether you assume the unfinished life or abandon it. Ripped from the novel (translated from the Russian by the cutter): «Значит, отдай ей свою.» — "'Then give her yours.'" And: «Любовь сделала вас с Устиной единым целым, а значит, часть Устины все еще здесь. Это – ты.» — "Love made you and Ustina a single whole, which means a part of Ustina is still here. That is you."

Why it matters: This is the engine that turns a bereaved healer into a saint. The novel's answer to irreversible loss is not acceptance but assumption: take the dead person's remaining life onto yourself and live it. It is the difference between being crushed by a loss and being conscripted by it.

Use when a loss — a death, a failed dream, an abandoned self — leaves an un-lived life behind. Ask not 'how do I get over it' but 'what part of it is still here, and am I carrying it?'

Arseny has the gift of healing, and the elder tells him bluntly: 'I won't spare you: you are guilty of her bodily death.' His gift saved everyone around him — the plague-stricken, the wounded, the dying — and it could not save the one person it was for. The mechanism: you become great at the thing precisely because it is useless for your one real wound. Competence as penance. The gift strengthens the more he sacrifices, but it is aimed at everyone except the one loss that matters. Ripped from the novel (translated from the Russian by the cutter): «Не буду тебя жалеть: ты виноват в ее смерти телесной.» — "I won't spare you: you are guilty of her bodily death."

Why it matters: This is the tragic motor of the whole book. The healing gift is not a consolation for Ustina's death; it is the wound's byproduct. It grows precisely where it cannot help her. What looks like virtue — endless service, endless sacrifice — is grief doing the only work it can find.

Use when you are becoming extremely good at something while the actual thing you wanted stays broken. Ask: is this excellence, or is this the wound learning to work around itself?

After Ustina dies, Arseny cannot bear the morning: 'the coming of a new day without Ustina was unbearable to him,' so he sleeps, refusing the day. That refusal is the first honest move grief makes — the day should not be allowed to come without her. The trap is freezing there: sleeping through the day instead of acting inside a present that still holds the dead. The elder's word is that where she is now 'there is no time, but there is the infinite mercy of God' — the dead are not in the past, they are present, so mourning never ends; it changes form. Refuse the new day, then act inside it. Don't sleep through it. Ripped from the novel (translated from the Russian by the cutter): «наступление нового дня без Устины было для него невыносимо» — "the coming of a new day without Ustina was unbearable to him." And: «И нет времени, а есть бесконечная милость Божия, на ню же уповаем.» — "And there is no time, but there is the infinite mercy of God, on which we rely."

Why it matters: This is the honest account of grief's first move: refusing the day is legitimate, sleeping through it is the trap. The dead do not recede — they remain. The question is only whether the refusal becomes a paralysis (sleep) or an engine (acting inside the present that still holds them).

Use when you or someone else is stuck on 'I can't believe it happened,' or sleeping the day away. The refusal is legitimate; staying frozen is the trap.

The four names are not a freedom — they are four renunciations. The book titles them: Knowledge, Renunciation, the Way, Rest. Each new self is bought by killing the previous one, and the whole arc is a житие — a saint's life with a predetermined sacred shape. The narrative that makes the suffering meaningful (a life lived as a hagiography) is the same structure that removes the freedom to be anything else. Vodolazkin's own framing says it: 'one cannot help being surprised that all these people bear a single name' — the single name is the cage that forces the several selves into one predestined story. Ripped from the novel (translated from the Russian by the cutter): «В таких случаях нельзя не испытывать удивления, что все эти люди носят одно имя.» — "In such cases one cannot help being surprised that all these people bear a single name."

Why it matters: This is the shadow side of card two. Substitution gives the grief a task, but the task hardens into a destiny. The cure (meaning) and the cage (closure) are the same structure. Arseny does not choose his life so much as he is chosen by the story he took on — and each of his four names is a self he had to die from.

Use when the story you tell about your life (redemption, penance, purpose) has stopped being a map and started being a sentence. Ask: am I using this narrative, or is it using me?

Before Ustina dies, she hands Arseny the substitution in advance, and he does not take it seriously: 'This shirt will be yours, only you must change your name. Not having the objective possibility of being Ustina, name yourself Ustin.' He says 'agreed,' finding her seriousness funny. Only after her death does he learn what he agreed to. The novel's mirror: the un-lived life is not always thrust on you by loss — sometimes the person hands it to you in advance, you accept it lightly, and only later discover you have been wearing it the whole time. Ask what you agreed to carry without understanding, and whose name you are now wearing. Ripped from the novel (translated from the Russian by the cutter): «эта рубаха будет твоей, только ты должен сменить имя. Не имея объективной возможности быть Устиной, нарекись Устином.» — "This shirt will be yours, only you must change your name. Not having the objective possibility of being Ustina, name yourself Ustin."

Why it matters: Flips the whole arc onto the reader. The substitution was not forced on Arseny; he consented to it before it mattered. That is the uncomfortable question: which dead person, abandoned dream, or past self did you promise to carry — and have you noticed that you are now living as them?

Use as the standing question of this deck. Use when you notice your life is shaped around someone who is gone, or a version of yourself you thought you'd left. Ask: whose name is on the shirt?
