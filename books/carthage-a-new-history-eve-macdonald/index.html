Card #c1183Synthesis

Silent trade: lay it out, step back, wait

"they [Carthaginians] come here and unload their cargo; then, having laid it out along the beach, they go back aboard their ships and light a smoking fire." "if it seems to them a fair price for their cargo, they take it and go away; but if not, they go back aboard and wait, and the people come back and add more gold until the sailors are satisfied."

— In our words · Source: Carthage: A New History (Eve MacDonald)

[Use when]

You have to strike a deal with no common language and no trust.

[Why it matters]

Herodotus describes Carthaginian traders dealing with a people whose language they didn't share. Each side set down its offer and withdrew. Nobody touched the other side's goods until one side accepted, so the turns did the negotiating.

Marry into the neighbours

"Success for the population at Carthage was largely dependent on the goodwill of the city’s neighbours for the first few centuries of its existence." "Hamilcar gave his daughter in marriage to Naravas, sealing the bond between the two men."

— In our words · Source: Carthage: A New History (Eve MacDonald)

[Use when]

You have moved into someone else's territory and depend on them for the basics.

[Why it matters]

For centuries Carthage ate mostly locally grown food and depended on the Libyans whose land it had settled. MacDonald credits intermarriage: the mixed rural families, the Libyphoenicians, farmed and fought for the city. The habit reached the top when Hamilcar married his daughter to the Numidian prince Naravas, who arrived with 2,000 cavalry.

Post the price list

"‘for each sacrifice which a person poor in cattle or in birds sacrifices the priests shall not receive [a thing]’" "‘any priest who takes a fee against what is set in this tablet shall be fined.’"

— In our words · Source: Carthage: A New History (Eve MacDonald)

[Use when]

You run something where insiders set the price and outsiders can't check it.

[Why it matters]

A tariff from the Temple of Ba'al Saphon, set by thirty men in charge of fees and dated by the sitting sufet, priced every offering from an ox down to flour and oil, in silver shekels. Clans, guilds and drinking clubs paid by the same list. The poor paid the priests nothing, and a priest who charged off the list was fined.
