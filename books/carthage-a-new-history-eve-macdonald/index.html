mechanism #1183

Silent trade: lay it out, step back, wait

Herodotus describes Carthaginian traders dealing with a people whose language they didn't share.

"they [Carthaginians] come here and unload their cargo; then, having laid it out along the beach, they go back aboard their ships and light a smoking fire." "if it seems to them a fair price for their cargo, they take it and go away; but if not, they go back aboard and wait, and the people come back and add more gold until the sailors are satisfied."

Why it matters

Herodotus describes Carthaginian traders dealing with a people whose language they didn't share. Each side set down its offer and withdrew. Nobody touched the other side's goods until one side accepted, so the turns did the negotiating.

Use when: You have to strike a deal with no common language and no trust.

mechanism #1189

mechanism #1191

Pay off the army in stages, in sight

Gisgo planned to bring the Sicilian army home in groups, paying each group off on arrival.

"Polybius records that Gisgo wanted to demobilise the Sicilian army in stages with each group being paid as they arrived in Carthage and then disbanded." "Removing the soldiers to Sicca was not a strategically sound decision."

Why it matters

Gisgo planned to bring the Sicilian army home in groups, paying each group off on arrival. The government let the men pile up unpaid in the city instead, then sent them to wait at Sicca, a region taxed and conscripted for 23 years. The soldiers asked where the generals who had made them promises were. The Mercenary War followed.

Use when: You owe a large group money you can't pay all at once.

mechanism #1192

Full authority in the field, full liability at home

A Carthaginian general made treaties, settled land, judged cases and struck coins without referring home, for as long as his war lasted.

"The Carthaginian commander in the field oversaw negotiating treaties, making decisions on settlements, judicial matters in camp and minting coinage." "The appointment to general was a whole project at which the commander either succeeded or failed."

Why it matters

A Carthaginian general made treaties, settled land, judged cases and struck coins without referring home, for as long as his war lasted. Then he answered for the result in person before a council of 104. Failure meant exile or crucifixion. Malchus, the first general on record, was prosecuted for treason.

mechanism #1194

Marry into the neighbours

For centuries Carthage ate mostly locally grown food and depended on the Libyans whose land it had settled.

"Success for the population at Carthage was largely dependent on the goodwill of the city’s neighbours for the first few centuries of its existence." "Hamilcar gave his daughter in marriage to Naravas, sealing the bond between the two men."

Why it matters

For centuries Carthage ate mostly locally grown food and depended on the Libyans whose land it had settled. MacDonald credits intermarriage: the mixed rural families, the Libyphoenicians, farmed and fought for the city. The habit reached the top when Hamilcar married his daughter to the Numidian prince Naravas, who arrived with 2,000 cavalry.

Use when: You have moved into someone else's territory and depend on them for the basics.

"The way it worked is that a ship would move from port to port, region to region, dropping off and picking up goods along the way, never straying far from land." "This shows a circular, counterclockwise navigation of the sea that accommodated winds and currents"

Why it matters

The Phoenician traders Carthage came from didn't cross open water in one run. They worked the coast in a loop that went with the winds and currents, selling and buying at every stop. Each leg was short, and each port was a market.

mechanism #1202

A harbour that shows visitors nothing

Appian describes two harbours sharing one entrance closed with iron chains.

"‘for a double wall enclosed them, and there were gates by which merchant ships could pass from the first port to the city without traversing the dockyards’" "It was built up to a height that allowed the admiral to observe what was going on out at sea, but anyone approaching by water did not have a clear view of the inside of the port."

Why it matters

Appian describes two harbours sharing one entrance closed with iron chains. Merchant ships used the rectangular port and passed into the city through gates. Warships sat in the circular port behind a double wall, 220 berths round an island, and the admiral's house on the island watched the sea while arriving ships saw nothing of the docks.

mechanism #1203

Build up when you can't build out

Hemmed in on every side, Carthage went up.

"The African style of building was a very stable construction method and was innovative as it allowed for walls that could bear a substantial load, and for the buildings to be built higher." "Carthage’s growth was constrained by geographical factors: the sea lay to the east, cemeteries to the north and southwest, and to the south, the Tophet."

Why it matters

Hemmed in on every side, Carthage went up. Its builders set upright stone blocks at intervals and filled between them with rubble or mud brick, which carried several storeys. The Romans admired the method and called it 'African style'.

mechanism #1207

Pick the general for war, the magistrate for office

In Rome you had to hold civil office before you could command an army.

"Here we can see that military duties were separated from the civic magistracies, which is unlike Rome where military leadership was predicated on holding a civil magistracy." "A military commander could go into the civic magistracies but did not necessarily evolve out of them."

Why it matters

In Rome you had to hold civil office before you could command an army. Carthage split the two tracks over time. Its general, the rab mahanet or head of the camp, was chosen for the war in front of him and held the job for that war, whatever his political career looked like.

Use when: Everyone assumes the person who runs the place should also lead the hard project.
