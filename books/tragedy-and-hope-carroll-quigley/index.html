Tragedy and Hope

Carroll Quigley · Nonfiction · 17 rips

Grand-strategy history — Quigley's 1966 classic on Western civilization's cycles, financial capitalism, the Anglo-American network, and the shape of the twentieth century.

Western Civilization, on the contrary, did not pass from the Age of Crisis to the Age of Universal Empire, but instead was able to reform itself and entered upon a new period of expansion. Moreover, Western Civilization did this not once, but several times. It was this ability to reform or reorganize itself again and again which made Western Civilization the dominant factor in the world at the beginning of the twentieth century.

Why it matters: Quigley's whole theory pivots here: every other civilization went expansion, crisis, universal empire, decay — the West broke the cycle by reorganizing itself, repeatedly, instead of consolidating into one empire.

Why it matters: A diagnostic checklist for civilizational stall: the economics slow first, then the politics turn violent, then the culture turns superstitious — in that order, core before periphery.

Why it matters: The engine of every Quigley cycle: the organization that created the expansion hardens into the thing blocking the next one — profit shifts from making more to guarding what exists.

Why it matters: Quigley's cleanest taxonomy: each stage of capitalism is defined by where profit is extracted — and each stage's profit source becomes the next stage's target for capture.

When classifying a business or an era — ask where the profit actually comes from, not what the brochure says.

Why it matters: The apex of Quigley's system in one paragraph: a private bank, owned by central banks that were themselves private corporations, settling the world's money by bookkeeping instead of shipping gold.
