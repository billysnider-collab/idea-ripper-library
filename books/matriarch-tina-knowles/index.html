Matriarch

by Tina Knowles

22 rips · Making things · Hiring and leading · Starting something

Building businesses and talent — Tina Knowles on opening Headliners salon, styling and co-managing Destiny's Child on a shoestring, and launching House of Deréon and Miss Tina.

Mechanisms (3)

mechanism #1367

A cheap first visit, if you trust the product

The stylists disliked giving away a thirty-dollar service.

"The flyer offered a $9.95 shampoo-and-blow-dry and reconstructive treatment for a first-time visit only—Mathew’s idea for a can’t-resist deal that would get people in the door." "I could do that because I was confident in the service we would provide—and the power of deep conditioning."

Why it matters

The stylists disliked giving away a thirty-dollar service. Knowles bet that one visit would convert women at the light, gas and water companies into regulars. At the grand opening she was booking days ahead. A loss-leader only works when the product sells itself on the second visit.

Use when: You're launching and debating whether to discount the first experience.

mechanism #1368

Belonging is the secret of branding

The front desk knew each client's last perm, her red or white wine, how she took her coffee.

"It was Mathew’s idea to put everyone on file in the database, which was unheard of in salons at the time. This would ensure that each client would feel important and remembered when she returned, because the secret of good branding is belonging…"

Why it matters

The front desk knew each client's last perm, her red or white wine, how she took her coffee. In the late 1980s that was rare for a salon. Remembering details at scale made every return visit feel personal.

Use when: Your customers come back and are treated like strangers each time.

Sing while you run

Performers have to sing while dancing, so the training combined both, the way athletes condition for game conditions.

"For them, Mathew developed summer “boot camp,” part of which involved taking them jogging along Brays Bayou. He had them sing the entire run, to increase their lung power while they did their choreography."

Why it matters

Performers have to sing while dancing, so the training combined both, the way athletes condition for game conditions. Practice built under harder conditions than the stage made the stage easier.

Use when: Your team rehearses each skill alone and then struggles when they all have to happen at once.

Arguments (4)

argument #1366

Find the need: time and respect

Headliners priced high and aimed at professional women because Knowles respected that their time wasn't cheap.

"Every woman I’d talked to hated that they’d sit in a salon for five hours—eating lunch under a dryer, listening to gossip—when they had a job or kids to get back to. I had found my niche, the consumer need that is vital to a new business: time and respect."

Why it matters

Headliners priced high and aimed at professional women because Knowles respected that their time wasn't cheap. Her business plan came from hours she and her friend Cheryl had wasted in salons. The niche was a complaint every potential customer already had.

Use when: You're looking for a business idea and keep thinking about products instead of what annoys your customers.

Label executives called the looks too bold and too "Motown" for white audiences.

"…when you’re sitting in the sixtieth row and you see some sequins up there, you feel like you’re in the front row because it’s bigger than life."

argument #1382

Pop will move to you

Sony said the group would never be as big as Britney, Jessica or Christina and blamed Knowles's styling.

"We didn’t think Destiny’s Child needed to move to pop, because pop would move to them. “Change the world,” I would tell them. “Don’t let the world change you.”"

Why it matters

Sony said the group would never be as big as Britney, Jessica or Christina and blamed Knowles's styling. She later saw white pop stars in rhinestones and camouflage couture copying Destiny's Child. A distinct look the market resists can become the look the market copies.

Use when: You're told to change your style to fit the mainstream.

argument #1385

Inclusive means the whole line

On HSN she showed the same item on three models of different sizes.

"I had always noticed that when clothing lines were “inclusive” of women who wore larger sizes, that meant they selected only one or two items to actually produce." "These pieces usually amounted to camouflage, heavy smocks of monotone-colored fabrics that conveyed to women that they needed to hide their bodies."

Why it matters

On HSN she showed the same item on three models of different sizes. She later moved Miss Tina to Walmart for prices everyone could afford and sizing every body could wear. A token gesture tells excluded customers exactly what you think of them.

Use when: You're extending a product to a new group of customers with a stripped-down version.

Evidence (1)

evidence #1379

The outfits you worry about are the ones that hit

Columbia gave her barely any wardrobe budget, so she bought fabric and designed costumes herself.

"I began designing clothes out of necessity, not because I had some dream of being a designer." "When I worried, “I’m going too far,” or “People aren’t going to understand this,” those were the outfits that got the most love from fans."

Why it matters

Columbia gave her barely any wardrobe budget, so she bought fabric and designed costumes herself. The label grew stingier as it realized "Mom" was making the clothes anyway. Her anxiety about going too far turned out to be a signal of the boldest, most loved work.

Use when: You're about to tone down a piece of work because it feels like too much.

passage #1370

Mix the old ritual with the new science

Her mother used hot olive oil, butter, mayonnaise and egg whites.

"“You can make the hair as strong as you want, Tenie, but it will pull right out if it’s not strong at the root.” At Headliners, I became a mixologist, honoring what had worked in the past but modernizing the methods."

Why it matters

Her mother used hot olive oil, butter, mayonnaise and egg whites. Knowles combined those moisture principles with high-end protein treatments because no single product on the market did both. The gap between tradition and technology became the salon's edge.

Use when: You're choosing between what has always worked and the newest method.

Stepping in would have fixed the moment and taught nothing. Knowles chose the harder answer, "You need to work harder," and her daughter thanked her for it later. Rescuing someone can cost them the lesson they need.

passage #1376

The ones who make it don't quit

Mathew asked Ed McMahon directly why the girls lost.

Why it matters

Mathew asked Ed McMahon directly why the girls lost. The answer was that they were too young and had more work to do. The loss became part of Destiny's Child's origin story, and Mathew took a formal co-manager role in response.

Use when: You've just lost publicly and need to decide what changes.

passage #1383

Turn the lights off

Sony had told Mathew his wife was limiting the group while the album was among the year's top sellers.

"He reached for the light switch and flicked it off. They sat in darkness for one beat, two beats. Three. He turned the switch on again. “Destiny’s Child is keeping the fucking lights on in here,” he said."

Why it matters

Sony had told Mathew his wife was limiting the group while the album was among the year's top sellers. He reminded executives who generated the revenue, dramatically enough that the moment became legendary. When you're carrying the numbers, make the other side feel it.

Use when: A partner who depends on your results is treating you as the problem.

passage #1387

Burnt roux, start over

Her mother taught the roux as a forty-five-minute rhythm of stirring and talking.

"…a burnt roux was ruin to your gumbo. If that happened, take the lesson, curse it while you washed the skillet and spoon, but start over."

Why it matters

Her mother taught the roux as a forty-five-minute rhythm of stirring and talking. Past a certain point no rescue works. The instruction is to accept the loss quickly, keep the lesson and begin again.

Use when: You're still trying to salvage something that went wrong too early to fix.

rule #1369

Everyone is special under the dryer

A doctor sat next to an opera singer and next to a young woman spending part of her first paycheck.

"Someone would always be at the ready to lead her to the dressing room to put on her smock, a gorgeous pink that leveled the playing field for all our clients." "There was no class system at the salon, no private green room that a VIP was whisked to. Everyone was special under the dryer."

Why it matters

A doctor sat next to an opera singer and next to a young woman spending part of her first paycheck. The uniform smock gave everyone the same experience. That made the room a networking space, and the price felt like an investment for the newcomer.

Use when: You're tempted to create a VIP tier that makes regular customers feel second class.

rule #1371

Hire fresh, and fire for gossip

New staff learned the culture before they were considered for a station.

"We got our stylists right out of beauty school, so none had developed bad habits." "This was not a place of judgment or gossip. If you were caught gossiping, you were fired. I would end up letting go of some people I really liked, but we just couldn’t have that kind of atmosphere."

Why it matters

New staff learned the culture before they were considered for a station. Knowles enforced the rule against people she liked, because tolerating gossip leads to fighting and backstabbing. Culture held because the rule cost something.

Use when: A talented person is quietly poisoning the atmosphere and you're hesitating.

rule #1373

You are your only competition

Nine-year-old Beyoncé lost her lead parts in Girls Tyme after straining her voice teaching everyone else.

"“That girl is not your competition. You are your only competition and that’s how it always will be. Focus on you and what you’re doing, and not on her. You gotta work harder.”"

Why it matters

Nine-year-old Beyoncé lost her lead parts in Girls Tyme after straining her voice teaching everyone else. Knowles refused to complain to the managers and pushed the voice lessons Beyoncé had been avoiding. Within about three months Beyoncé had her spot back. She later traced the song "Survivor" to that lesson.

Use when: Someone you're coaching wants you to fight a battle they could win by getting better.

rule #1384

Give them the facts and let them choose

When Sony pushed a famous stylist for the "Independent Women" video, Knowles offered to step aside.

"But this time, Mathew had told them exactly what they said about me and their look so they could make an informed decision themselves."

Why it matters

When Sony pushed a famous stylist for the "Independent Women" video, Knowles offered to step aside. The group refused. Because they had the full picture, the choice was theirs, and their loyalty carried weight.
