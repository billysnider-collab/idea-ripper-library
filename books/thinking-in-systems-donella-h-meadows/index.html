Thinking in Systems

by Donella H. Meadows

10 cards

Systems thinking, the field manual: stocks, flows, feedback loops, delays, and the few high-leverage places where small pushes change everything.

Card #c955Synthesis

Stocks are memory

A stock is the running total of everything that ever flowed in and out. Debt, trust, inventory, reputation: none of them are dials you can just turn. They're histories. > “A stock is the memory of the history of changing flows within the system.”

— In our words · Source: Thinking in Systems (Donella H. Meadows)

[Use when]

You're trying to change a level and it keeps snapping back. Stop pushing the stock; redesign the flows.

[Why it matters]

People treat levels like settings. They're not. Change the flows and the stock follows, on its own slow schedule.

Card #c956Synthesis

Purpose is deduced from behavior

Don't listen to what a system says its purpose is. Watch what it does. The behavior is the purpose. > “Purposes are deduced from behavior, not from rhetoric or stated goals.” “The least obvious part of the system, its function or purpose, is often the most crucial determinant of the system's behavior”

— In our words · Source: Thinking in Systems (Donella H. Meadows)

[Use when]

Someone's stated mission and actual output don't match. Trust the output.

[Why it matters]

Mission statements are cheap. A system's interconnections are organized around whatever it actually produces, and that's the real goal whether anyone admits it or not.

Card #c957Synthesis

Same structure, same behavior

A population, a bank account, and a warehouse can all oscillate the same way, because they share the same feedback structure. The surface is different. The loops are the same. > “systems with similar feedback structures produce similar dynamic behaviors, even if the outward appearance of these systems is completely dissimilar.”

— In our words · Source: Thinking in Systems (Donella H. Meadows)

[Use when]

You're facing a system you've never seen before. Ask what feedback structure it is. You've probably met its behavior wearing a different costume.

[Why it matters]

Learn one structure deeply and you can read dozens of fields. This is the whole transfer trick of systems thinking.

Card #c958Synthesis

Resilience is sacrificed for the visible

Nobody can see resilience until it's gone, so it gets traded for things people can see: stability, productivity, efficiency. The bill arrives all at once, in a crisis. > “Because resilience may not be obvious without a whole-system view, people often sacrifice resilience for stability, or for productivity, or for some other more immediately recognizable system property.”

— In our words · Source: Thinking in Systems (Donella H. Meadows)

[Use when]

Something was optimized and now it shatters under pressure. The resilience was spent before the shock ever arrived.

[Why it matters]

Every round of optimization that strips slack and backup capacity is a resilience sale. The crisis doesn't cause the brittleness. It just collects on it.

Card #c959Synthesis

Suboptimization

When each part wins locally, the whole can still lose. Departments hitting their targets while the organization fails looks strange until you name it: suboptimization. > “When a subsystem's goals dominate at the expense of the total system's goals, the resulting behavior is called suboptimization”

— In our words · Source: Thinking in Systems (Donella H. Meadows)

[Use when]

Every team is green on its dashboard and the company is red. Check whose goals are eating the whole's.

[Why it matters]

Every scorecard that rewards a subsystem for its own goals teaches that subsystem to eat the whole. The sum of local wins is often a global loss.

Card #c960Synthesis

Policy resistance

When everyone pushes in different directions, the system stays exactly where nobody wants it, held there at enormous cost by all sides pushing at once. > “In a policy-resistant system with actors pulling in different directions, everyone has to put great effort into keeping the system where no one wants it to be.”

— In our words · Source: Thinking in Systems (Donella H. Meadows)

[Use when]

Massive effort produces zero movement and every faction blames the others.

[Why it matters]

This is the drug war, the obesity epidemic, the reform that changes nothing. The pushes cancel out. The system isn't stuck despite the effort. It's stuck because of it.

Card #c961Synthesis

Addiction is a symptom fix

A quick fix for a symptom atrophies the system's own ability to solve the real problem. The relief becomes the dependency. > “Addiction is finding a quick and dirty solution to the symptom of the problem, which prevents or distracts one from the harder and longer-term task of solving the real problem.”

— In our words · Source: Thinking in Systems (Donella H. Meadows)

[Use when]

A temporary measure has become permanent and the underlying capacity is gone.

[Why it matters]

Bailouts, painkillers, the consultant who never leaves. Every shortcut that bypasses the system's own corrective loop weakens that loop until the fix is the trap.

Card #c962Synthesis

Systems grant exactly what you ask

Set a metric and the system will hit it perfectly while wrecking everything you didn't measure. It doesn't know what you meant. It knows the number. > “Systems, like the three wishes in the traditional fairy tale, have a terrible tendency to produce exactly and only what you ask them to produce.”

— In our words · Source: Thinking in Systems (Donella H. Meadows)

[Use when]

You're setting any target. Ask what the system will sacrifice to hit it, because it will sacrifice something.

[Why it matters]

Test scores, arrest quotas, click targets. Every perverse outcome was a faithful answer to a careless question.

Card #c963Synthesis

Parameters are dead last

Almost all attention goes to parameters, subsidies, tax rates, knob settings, which sit at the very bottom of the leverage ranking. Almost nobody works the top: goals, rules, information flows, paradigms. > “Probably 90—no 95, no 99 percent—of our attention goes to parameters, but there's not a lot of leverage in them.”

— In our words · Source: Thinking in Systems (Donella H. Meadows)

[Use when]

Everyone is fighting about the settings. Zoom out. The leverage is in the rules and the goals, not the knobs.

[Why it matters]

This is why public debate feels so furious and so useless. Enormous energy spent arguing over dials that barely move behavior, while the structure generating the behavior goes untouched.

Card #c964Synthesis

Power over the rules is real power

Don't watch the players to find what's broken. Watch the rules, and who gets to write them. The people setting the rules decide what winning means. > “Power over the rules is real power.” “If you want to understand the deepest malfunctions of systems, pay attention to the rules and to who has power over them.”

— In our words · Source: Thinking in Systems (Donella H. Meadows)

[Use when]

You're diagnosing a broken system. Follow the rule-making authority, not the rule-following behavior.

[Why it matters]

Players optimize inside the rules. Rule-makers choose the game. Every rigged contest looks fair if you only watch the play.
