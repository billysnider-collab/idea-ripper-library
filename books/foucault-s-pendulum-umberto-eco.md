## Steal

There are four kinds of people in this world: cretins, fools, morons, and lunatics.

A lunatic is easily recognized. He is a moron who doesn’t know the ropes. The moron proves his thesis; he has a logic, however twisted it may be. The lunatic, on the other hand, doesn’t concern himself at all with logic; he works by short circuits. For him, everything proves everything else.

Everything I’ve said to you so far is false. Good night, Casaubon.

## Why it matters

Belbo's bar-room taxonomy is the novel's engine: the lunatic is the figure who reads every event as confirmation of the secret he already carries. It explains why the Diabolicals — and the three editors who mock them — can never argue anyone out of a conspiracy: logic was never the binding agent.

## Use when

Use when you meet someone for whom every new fact is further proof of the old theory, and you need to name the mechanism instead of arguing the facts.

The novel's central mechanism: a conspiracy is assembled as a joke with a word processor and some connective tissue — and the machine keeps producing it because the connective tissue does all the work. The content is arbitrary; the grammar of proof is the product.

## Steal

Belbo decided to take the universe of the Diabolicals seriously, not because of an abundance of faith, but because of a total lack of it.

he came to realize that by inventing the Plan he had actually created. He fell in love with his golem, found it a source of consolation.

Fear forced him to be brave. Inventing, he had created the principle of reality.

## Why it matters

Belbo's tragedy: a man too skeptical to believe anything discovers that invention is a form of belief — and the invented thing becomes more real than his own life, because others start acting on it. The void does not stay empty; it gets filled by whatever you built to mock it.

With numbers you can do anything you like.

How did I arrive at this? I divided thirteen hundred and fourteen by two, by three, et cetera, until I found a satisfying date.

## Why it matters

Agliè demolishes numerology by performing it: measure a lottery kiosk, divide until a 'satisfying date' appears, and any object encodes the cosmos. The method's honesty is the point — every numerological proof confesses that the operator chose the target first.

## Steal

Belbo recovered his most genuine gift: his sense of the ridiculous.

And Belbo, now invincible, said, “Ma gavte la nata...”

Thus Belbo had escaped the error of the world and its movements, had now become, himself, the point of suspension, the Fixed Pin, the Place from which the vault of the world is hung, while beneath his feet the wire and the sphere went on swinging, from pole to pole, without peace, the earth slipping away under them, showing always a new continent.

## Why it matters

At the gallows Belbo refuses the one thing that would save him — revealing a secret he knows is empty — and his refusal is pure style: a Piedmontese insult, a sense of the ridiculous. Dying, he becomes what the pendulum's mathematics always promised: the still point everything else rotates around.

## Use when

Use when the only dignified move is to refuse the game entirely — to hold your silence and your scorn at the same time.

## Steal

The initiate encourages the mystic and uses him as you might use a telephone, to establish long-distance contact, or as a chemist might use litmus paper, to detect the action of a particular substance. The mystic is useful, because he is conspicuous. He broadcasts himself. Initiates, on the contrary, are recognizable only to one another.

Mysticism is a democratic, if not demagogic, phenomenon; initiation is aristocratic.

## Why it matters

Agliè's distinction inverts the usual hierarchy: the ecstatic who performs his possession is the instrument, not the master — a telephone, litmus paper. The real operators stay invisible and let the loud ones transmit. It is a theory of power as ventriloquism.

## Use when

Use when the loudest believer in a movement is being used as a signal by someone quieter.

“From prohibitions you can tell what people normally do,” Belbo said. “It’s a way of drawing a picture of daily life.”

## Why it matters
