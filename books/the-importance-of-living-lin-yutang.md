## Steal

"Reality + Dreams + Humor = Wisdom" — Lin's calculus. Dreams (imagination, aspiration, idealism) are the fuel of life; humor is the regulator that keeps them from curdling into fanaticism; reality is the ballast. Reality without dreams is mere animal existence; dreams without humor harden into fanaticism; dreams and humor without reality are empty fantasy; reality and humor without dreams are dead conservatism. Wisdom is the three held together.

## Why it matters

Every movement, plan, and person can be diagnosed by asking which term is missing. The zealot has dreams and certainty but no humor; the cynic has reality and humor but no dreams; the naif has dreams and humor but no reality. Check your own ratio before you trust your own convictions.

## Use when

A cause, a pitch, or your own enthusiasm starts feeling airtight — look for the missing term before it costs you.

## Steal

"Human dignity must be associated with the idea of a scamp and not with that of an obedient, disciplined and regimented soldier." Man is "the greatest scamp on earth" — curious, dreamy, humorous, wayward. The scamp can't be standardized: he plays, dreams, laughs, and refuses the uniform. His waywardness isn't a flaw to be trained out; it's the proof he hasn't been fully domesticated by institutions. Regimes fear him precisely because he cannot be regimented.

## Why it matters

Every institution — schools, armies, corporations, parties — quietly tries to replace the scamp with the soldier as its ideal human product. Lin's test: "only he who handles his ideas lightly is master of his ideas, and only he who is master of his ideas is not enslaved by them." Seriousness is effort; lightness is mastery. Measure a person or an organization by how much scamp survives in it.

## Use when

You catch yourself (or your organization) rewarding obedience and discipline while quietly punishing curiosity, playfulness, and waywardness.

## Steal

"Any good practical philosophy must start out with the recognition of our having a body." Lin calls this reasonable naturalism: passions and desires are neither good nor bad in themselves — they are simply given. The civilized task is to bring appetite, feeling, intellect, and conscience into harmony, not to purge the flesh as medieval Christians and Buddhists tried to do. Spirit that scorns the body becomes inhuman; a program of life that ignores the body is not noble, it's abstract.

## Why it matters

Most moral and productivity systems are amputation systems: they declare whole parts of human nature (hunger, laziness, desire, play) as enemies and then demand willpower to fight them. Lin's alternative — expect neither too much nor too little of man, harmonize rather than deny — is cheaper, kinder, and actually works, because it starts from the creature that exists rather than the angel that doesn't.

## Use when

A philosophy, diet, or productivity doctrine asks you to declare war on your own body — try tuning the appetites instead of executing them.

## Steal

"Every funeral procession carries a banner upon which are written the words, 'Equality of Mankind.'" Death dissolves every hierarchy — the king's thousand acres buy him the same five-foot bed as the peasant's. Because everyone dies, ownership is temporary and status is theater. This doesn't sadden life; it concentrates it. Mortality is the mechanism that turns earthly existence into the only show that matters: "I am therefore as good as a king," because the king and I both end up the same size.

## Why it matters

The fear of death is routinely exploited to sell you urgency, ambition, and accumulation — as if frantic achievement could outrun the banner. Lin inverts it: accepting death is what frees you to enjoy what's actually here. A vague hope of immortality "detracts from our wholehearted enjoyment" of the only life that's certain.

## Use when

Ambition starts selling you the idea that you must become immortal-by-achievement — remember the funeral banner and price the present correctly.

## Steal

"Philosophy not only begins with the individual, but also ends with the individual." The person is the final fact of life — "an end in himself, and not a means to other creations of the human mind." Lin's test: the greatest empire on earth exists so that an ordinary Englishman in Sussex "may live a fairly happy and reasonable life." A false philosophy inverts it and assumes the man in Sussex exists so that there may be a great empire. States, institutions, and civilizations are judged by one metric: do individual lives get happier and more reasonable?

## Why it matters

Every abstract system — state, party, class, market, ideology — quietly tries to turn persons into fuel. Lin's doctrine is the acid test that dissolves them all: if the scheme doesn't terminate in a happier individual life, it's a machine for consuming people. This is the argument to reach for whenever someone asks you to sacrifice yourself for a collective abstraction.

## Use when

An institution, ideology, or "greater good" argument asks you to be the means — demand proof that you, the individual, are the end.

## Steal

"We should lead a decent human life simply because we are decent human beings" — the line that cut Lin's last tie to Christianity. Morality built on heaven-and-hell bookkeeping cheapens morality itself: it implies you wouldn't be decent without the reward, and it drags goodness through a gauntlet of hypothetical postulates — sin, redemption, celestial treasure — none capable of direct proof. Paganism's simplicity: doing good is its own justification. Love among men should be a final, absolute fact, without being reminded of a third party in heaven.

## Why it matters

Transaction-based morality is fragile morality. If your decency depends on cosmic wages, it collapses the moment the wages look uncertain — and it insults the dignity of the act itself. Lin's pagan standard is sturdier: reverence for the beauty, grandeur, and mystery of life is sufficient, and the orphan who has learned to be independent is more mature than the child waiting for a father's presents.

## Use when

Someone frames ethics as a cosmic loyalty program — rewards for the good, punishments for the bad — and you need the case that decency stands on its own feet.

## Steal

"All human happiness is biological happiness... all human happiness is sensuous happiness." The material/spiritual split is a fraud: the partition separating flesh from spirit is extremely thin. Digestion, sleep, a cool spring on a parched throat, the morning air after sleep — these aren't crude preludes to the "real" pleasures. Art, affection, music, even religious feeling all arrive through the senses. Deny the body and you get a world with "knowledge without understanding, criticism without appreciation, beauty without love, truth without passion."

## Why it matters

Refined people love to be embarrassed by their senses, treating bodily pleasure as vulgar and "higher" pleasures as disembodied. Lin argues the reverse: the higher pleasures are just the senses, cultivated. Taste, hearing, touch, appetite — trained, not transcended — are the actual machinery of enjoyment. Asceticism doesn't refine you; it disconnects you from your own instruments.

## Use when

Culture or religion tells you to feel guilty about enjoying food, comfort, music, or the morning air — the senses are the instrument, not the enemy.

## Steal

"Leisure in time is like unoccupied floor space in a room." Unused time is to life what unused floor space is to a room — the part that makes everything else usable. Fill every inch with efficiency and there's no room to move; the girl in the room where every inch is utilized feels cramped, not rich. Culture, judgment, friendship, and taste aren't byproducts of production — they're products of loafing. Efficiency, punctuality, and the hunger for achievement are the three American vices precisely because they pave over the empty space where life happens.

## Why it matters

The productivity frame treats unoccupied time as waste to be optimized. Lin's mechanism says the opposite: emptiness is the enabling condition. A schedule with no slack can't produce judgment, a life with no idleness can't produce culture, and a person who can't loaf gracefully can't think clearly. Protect the empty floor the way you'd protect the foundation of the house.

## Use when

Your calendar is fully optimized and your life feels cramped — you don't need better time management; you need deliberately unoccupied time.

## Steal

"The aim of education or culture is merely the development of good taste in knowledge and good form in conduct." The cultured person is not the well-read one — "one who likes and dislikes the right things." Erudition without discernment is just facts without judgment; modern education rewards memorization because facts are easy to test and taste is not. Real culture is independent judgment with the courage to call fashionable, academic, political, or prestigious humbug by its name.

## Why it matters

The credential game optimizes for storage: degrees, reading lists, diplomas. Lin argues the whole game is measuring the wrong thing — knowledge should be a personal adventure of likes and dislikes, not compliance. A person with taste can navigate a flood of information; a person with only storage drowns in it while looking impressive.

## Use when

Judging an education, a hire, or your own learning — ask about taste and judgment, not the size of the warehouse.

## Steal

"The logical man is always self-righteous and therefore inhuman and therefore wrong, while the reasonable man suspects that perhaps he is wrong and is therefore always right." Logic deprived of common sense becomes inhuman: it pushes every theory doggedly to its absurd conclusion and erects the state, the class, or the system into a god that swallows the individual. Reasonableness — "humanized thinking" — rounds out ideas and tones down their angularities. In human affairs the illogical course is often the convincing one: the postscript that contradicts the letter, the pardon that voids the argument, the unanswerable case answered with compassion.

## Why it matters

Most modern disputes are fought with flawless logic on both sides and zero reasonableness — which is why they never end. The mechanism Lin names: certainty is the tell. The person who is entirely sure is the person the argument has captured; the person with a flash of doubt in the postscript is the one still thinking. Fanaticism, left or right, is not a doctrine problem — it's a reasonableness deficit.

## Use when

An argument is airtight and everyone is still miserable — the logic isn't wrong, it's inhuman; look for the reasonable postscript.
