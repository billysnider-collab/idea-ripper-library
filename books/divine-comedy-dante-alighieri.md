## Steal

Treat disorientation as the real beginning: you can't climb out of a bad situation until you first admit the straight path is lost. The admission, not the escape plan, is step one.

## Why it matters

The poem opens not with a plan but with a confession: "Midway upon the journey of our life / I found myself within a forest dark, / For the straightforward pathway had been lost." The dark wood is the symptom; the lost pathway is the problem — the loss of orientation itself. Everything after depends on this admission: he can't even see the hill of escape clearly until he says plainly that he's lost. The first truthful sentence is the real beginning.

## Use when

You (or someone you're helping) keeps trying to fix a problem without naming what actually went wrong, and every fix circles back to the same place.

## Steal

Design consequences (for yourself, a team, a product) that mirror the behavior, so the consequence reveals what the behavior actually was, stripped of its excuse.

## Why it matters

Dante names the rule in Inferno XXVIII, out of the mouth of the poet who sowed discord: "Thus is observed in me the counterpoise" — Longfellow's rendering of contrapasso. Every punishment mirrors the sin: the sin stripped of its self-justification, made visible and permanent. It runs both directions: read a punishment and you can reconstruct exactly what the sin was, because the punishment is the sin seen honestly. A consequence shaped like the action is self-documenting. It teaches without a lecture.

## Use when

You're deciding how to respond to a repeated failure or bad behavior, and you want the consequence to teach, not just punish, or you want to see what a flaw looks like from outside.

## Steal

Watch for the moment hope (the ability to imagine repair) is abandoned, because that is the real point of no return, in a person, a team, or a project.

## Why it matters

The inscription over Hell's gate lists what Hell is made of, then lands the point: "All hope abandon, ye who enter in." The horror of Hell is not the pain — it's that the pain no longer leads anywhere. Hope, the ability to picture a way out, is the first thing damned. The tell in life is the same: a team that stops proposing fixes isn't short on ideas. It has already abandoned the hope that fixes work.

## Use when

You're trying to tell whether a situation is still fixable, or whether the people in it have already stopped believing it can be fixed.

## Steal

Distinguish love from being carried: if it never lets you stand still, choose a direction, or rest, it may be wind, not love. The feeling that carries you away is the tell.

## Why it matters

The second circle's punishment is exact: "The infernal hurricane that never rests / Hurtles the spirits onward in its rapine." The sinners are not resting together. They are blown — never able to stop, choose, or land. The passion that governed them is now shown for what it was: being governed. The contrapasso names the distinction love tries to blur. Love chooses. Being blown doesn't. Love can stand still. The wind can't.

## Use when

You're looking at a relationship, habit, or obsession that feels passionate but keeps you from self-direction, and you want to tell attachment from being swept along.

## Steal

Catch the tell of displaced responsibility: when someone (or you) says a feeling did it, the feeling isn't the agent. Assigning agency to the emotion is the tell that a choice is being disowned.

## Why it matters

Francesca's account is self-exoneration from end to end: "Love, that exempts no one beloved from loving, / Seized me with pleasure... Love has conducted us unto one death." Love is the subject of every verb. She is the object — seized, conducted, exempted. She never once appears as the one who chose. Then she blames the book: "Galeotto was the book and he who wrote it." The tell is grammatical: when the emotion is the subject and the person is the object, a choice is being smuggled into a description.

## Use when

You hear 'I couldn't help it,' 'love made me,' or 'I was carried away,' and you want to tell an explanation from an excuse.

## Steal

When two people are screaming opposite things at each other about the same object, suspect they share the same underlying problem, and the fight is the point, not the resolution.

## Why it matters

The fourth circle puts two groups in one punishment, each rolling weights and shouting the opposite charge — keep versus spend. The hoarder and the waster accuse each other, but they roll the same weight in the same circle; the difference is only direction. Both are slaves of money. The fight lets each side feel different from the other. A perfectly symmetrical fight, where each side is the other's mirror image, is usually one problem wearing two costumes.

## Use when

Two people or factions are locked in an endless, symmetrical fight over the same thing, and you want to see the shared root instead of picking a side.

## Steal

Self-destruction doesn't end the self, it deforms it: you can't throw away the thing and keep it. What you destroy in yourself, you lose the use of.

## Why it matters

The contrapasso here is stark: "Men once we were, and now are changed to trees." The ones who discarded their bodies never get them back — no human form, no human voice; they only speak when their branches are broken, bleeding words and blood together. The logic holds in life: the part of yourself you destroy to escape the pain is the part you lose the use of. Self-destruction is sold as an ending, but it's really a conversion. The self doesn't vanish. It becomes a thing.

## Use when

You (or someone) is burning out, self-sabotaging, or destroying a part of themselves to escape, and you want to name what the destruction actually costs.

## Steal

Rank wrongs by their temperature: the cold, deliberate, premeditated wrong is worse than the hot, reactive one. Betrayal beats rage, and it should be treated accordingly.

## Why it matters

At the bottom of Hell, beneath even the violent, the treacherous sit "within the ice," in a lake that is "glass, and not of water." Betrayal is cold, so its punishment is cold made solid — frozen at the coldest distance from the warmth of trust. Dante's ranking is deliberate: fraud and betrayal sit lower than violence, because violence can be hot and impulsive, but betrayal is cold and chosen.

## Use when

You're deciding how seriously to take a wrong, or someone is lumping a cold betrayal in with a hot mistake, and you want a principled ranking.

## Steal

The difference between a trap and a practice is naming: the same flaw, once named, becomes work you can do on yourself instead of a loop you're stuck in.

## Why it matters

In Purgatory the proud carry the stone that does what pride refused to do: "And were I not impeded by the stone, / Which this proud neck of mine doth subjugate, / Whence I am forced to hold my visage down." The bending is voluntary and purgative, not punitive: "And here must I this burden bear for it." The difference between Hell and Purgatory is not the sin — it's the posture toward it. Hell makes the sin permanent. Purgatory makes it a task.

## Use when

You're stuck in a pattern and can't tell whether you're trapped or working on it, and you want the criterion that separates the two.

## Steal

The cure for envy is not wanting less, it's needing more: force yourself to depend on the very people you envy, and the envy has nothing to feed on.

## Why it matters

The envious carry the two wounds envy inflicts: "For all their lids an iron wire transpierces," sewing shut the eyes that once coveted others' fortune — and they lean: "And one sustained the other with his shoulder." The contrapasso is also the prescription: envy is a problem of the eyes and the self. Shut the covetous gaze and reopen dependence. Envy dissolves not by wanting less but by needing the other. Once you depend on someone, their good is your good.

## Use when

You feel envy toward someone and want to actually dissolve it, rather than just suppress it or feel bad about it.
