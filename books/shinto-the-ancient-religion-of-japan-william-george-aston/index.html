Shinto: The Ancient Religion of Japan

by William George Aston

15 cards

Japanese religion — Aston's 1921 classic on Shinto: nature-gods, ritual purity, myth, and the making of the Imperial cult.

Card #c209Synthesis

Shinto is not ancestor-worship

It is quite true that there is a large element of ancestor worship in modern Japanese religious practices, but a very little examination shows that all the great deities of the older Shinto are not Man, but Nature gods.

— In our words · Source: Shinto: The Ancient Religion of Japan (William George Aston)

[Use when]

When a belief about a tradition comes mostly from its modern packaging, check the founding layer — the ancestors may be younger than the gods.

[Why it matters]

Corrects the most durable misunderstanding of Shinto — held even by most educated Japanese in Aston's day — by naming what the old gods actually were: Sun, Moon, Earth, Sea, Rain-storm, Fire, Thunder.

Card #c210Synthesis

A religion of love, not fear

It is rather, as Schiller called the worship of the gods of Greece, a Wonnedienst, a religion inspired by love and gratitude more than by fear.

— In our words · Source: Shinto: The Ancient Religion of Japan (William George Aston)

[Use when]

When explaining why a religion endures, test the fear hypothesis against the love hypothesis — Shinto's staying power came from gratitude.

[Why it matters]

Flips the default anthropology assumption — the saying 'Fear first made the Gods' does not hold here; the three greatest gods are beneficent beings and their festivals are occasions of rejoicing.

Card #c211Synthesis

Kami are high, not infinite

The Kami are high, swift, good, rich, living, but not infinite, omnipotent, or omniscient.

— In our words · Source: Shinto: The Ancient Religion of Japan (William George Aston)

[Use when]

When a system's gods have limits, read the religion as a technology for managing superiors rather than a metaphysics of the absolute.

[Why it matters]

Defines the operating range of Shinto divinity: powerful superiors rather than an all-everything God — which is why they can be offended, propitiated, and kept at a ritual distance.

Card #c212Synthesis

Motoori's definition of Kami

Not only human beings, but birds, beasts, plants and trees, seas and mountains, and all other things whatsoever which deserve to be dreaded and revered for the extraordinary and pre-eminent powers which they possess, are called Kami.

— In our words · Source: Shinto: The Ancient Religion of Japan (William George Aston)

[Use when]

When deciding what counts as a 'god' in someone else's religion, use their criterion (dread-worthy power), not yours (moral perfection).

[Why it matters]

The widest working definition of divinity in the book — dread and reverence for extraordinary power, with no requirement of goodness, nobleness, or serviceableness.

Card #c213Synthesis

Gods with no characters

They are very feebly characterised. Indeed, most of them may be said to have no characters at all.

— In our words · Source: Shinto: The Ancient Religion of Japan (William George Aston)

[Use when]

When a pantheon feels thin, consider that weak characterization is the feature: gods as addressable powers, not characters.

[Why it matters]

Aston's claim that the ancient Japanese imagination produced hundreds of deities but almost no personalities — polytheism without portraiture, distinct from the Greek model.

Card #c214Synthesis

Gods die of being forgotten

There is a constant depletion in their ranks by the mere force of oblivion, while, on the other hand, new deities come into notice.

— In our words · Source: Shinto: The Ancient Religion of Japan (William George Aston)

[Use when]

When tracking which ideas survive in an organization, watch attention the way Shinto watches worship — what stops being noticed dies.

[Why it matters]

Worship as the life-support system of gods: without attention they sink into oblivion and new ones surface — divinity as an attention economy.

Card #c215Synthesis

The Sun-goddess is no autocrat

Yet she is not what we should call a Supreme Deity. She is by no means an autocrat.

— In our words · Source: Shinto: The Ancient Religion of Japan (William George Aston)

[Use when]

When power looks absolute at the top, check for the council — Shinto's heaven is governed by committee, not decree.

[Why it matters]

Even heaven's ruler — unrivalled in dignity, Ruler of Heaven — shares power with a Council of the Gods that decides important matters; Shinto's cosmos runs on distributed authority.

Card #c216Synthesis

The mirror that is never opened

It is kept in a bag of brocade which is never opened or repaired, a new one being added on the top of its predecessor when the latter is too much worn for further use.

— In our words · Source: Shinto: The Ancient Religion of Japan (William George Aston)

[Use when]

When a system's authority rests on something no one may inspect, expect the rule 'add a layer, never open' — that is how sacred objects stay sacred.

[Why it matters]

Reverence maintained by refusing verification — the Sun-goddess's shintai at Ise is preserved by layering new wrappings over old, never looking inside.

Card #c217Synthesis

Priests with no cure of souls

They have no 'cure of souls,' and their duties are confined to reading the litanies and seeing to the repairs of the shrine.

— In our words · Source: Shinto: The Ancient Religion of Japan (William George Aston)

[Use when]

When a role looks priestly, check the job description — Shinto's kannushi maintains the building and reads the liturgy; souls are someone else's department.

[Why it matters]

The Shinto priest as facilities manager, not shepherd — appointed by the civil authorities, indistinguishable from the laity off duty; religion as maintenance, not ministry.

Card #c218Synthesis

Shrine means palace; festival means government

Miya meant equally shrine and palace. Matsuri, a Shinto festival, is the same word that we also find in Matsurigoto, government.

— In our words · Source: Shinto: The Ancient Religion of Japan (William George Aston)

[Use when]

When analyzing a system where religion and state seem fused, look at the vocabulary — if one word does both jobs, the separation was never real.

[Why it matters]

The sacred and the secular were never separated in ancient Japan — the language itself fuses worship and rule, with the Mikado as high priest and sovereign at once.

Card #c219Synthesis

Prayers are for material blessings only

Shinto prayers are for material blessings only.

— In our words · Source: Shinto: The Ancient Religion of Japan (William George Aston)

[Use when]

When reading a prayer, note what it asks for — Shinto's ask list is entirely material, which tells you what the religion is for.

[Why it matters]

Rain in drought, good harvests, health, long life to the sovereign — the norito liturgies petition for outcomes in this world, never for salvation or the soul.

Card #c220Synthesis

The offence was ritual, the Jewish moral

It is the crime of taking away human life which is condemned in the Decalogue: the Ohoharahi objects to wounds as nasty, unsightly things, unmeet for a God to look upon or to be in any way associated with. In a word, the Japanese offence was ritual, the Jewish moral.

— In our words · Source: Shinto: The Ancient Religion of Japan (William George Aston)

[Use when]

When two rules look alike, compare the motive underneath — same prohibition, ritual motive versus moral motive, completely different religions.

[Why it matters]

The sharpest one-line comparative ethics in the book — self-inflicted wounds, the cutting of dead bodies, and justifiable homicide all polluted equally, because pollution is about unsightliness before the gods, not wrongness.

Card #c221Synthesis

No ethics code as proof of goodness

Shintoists do not deny this feature of their religion, but claim that the absence of a code of ethics is a proof of the superior natural goodness of the Japanese nation. It needs no such artificial aids to virtuous conduct.

— In our words · Source: Shinto: The Ancient Religion of Japan (William George Aston)

[Use when]

When someone defends the absence of a rule as proof of virtue, ask what happens when the virtue is tested — Aston spent the whole chapter showing the gap.

[Why it matters]

The most audacious defense of a missing moral code in the book — the absence of ethics reframed as evidence of natural virtue rather than a gap.

Card #c222Synthesis

Buddhism swallowed the gods as avatars

They discovered that whether Nature-gods or Man-gods they were nothing more than avatars or incarnations of the various Buddhas.

— In our words · Source: Shinto: The Ancient Religion of Japan (William George Aston)

[Use when]

When a new system wants to win without a fight, watch for reclassification — the old gods survive by becoming avatars of the new ones.

[Why it matters]

The mechanism of religious conquest by absorption — Buddhist missionaries did not refute Shinto's gods, they reclassified them as local forms of Buddhas, producing Ryobu Shinto.

Card #c223Synthesis

Shinto is doomed to extinction

Without a code of morals, or an efficient ecclesiastical organisation, with little aid from the arts of painting, sculpture, and architecture, and with a sacred literature scanty and feeble compared with those of its foreign rivals, Shinto is doomed to extinction.

— In our words · Source: Shinto: The Ancient Religion of Japan (William George Aston)

[Use when]

When an expert declares a living tradition dead, keep the receipt — Aston wrote the obituary in 1921 and the patient survived him.

[Why it matters]

Aston's 1921 closing verdict — delivered with total confidence, and worth keeping as a card precisely because history gets to grade it: the religion he buried is still alive.
