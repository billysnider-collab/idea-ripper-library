mechanism #214

Gods die of being forgotten

Worship as the life-support system of gods: without attention they sink into oblivion and new ones surface — divinity as an attention economy.

There is a constant depletion in their ranks by the mere force of oblivion, while, on the other hand, new deities come into notice.

Why it matters

Worship as the life-support system of gods: without attention they sink into oblivion and new ones surface — divinity as an attention economy.

Use when: When tracking which ideas survive in an organization, watch attention the way Shinto watches worship — what stops being noticed dies.

mechanism #222

Buddhism swallowed the gods as avatars

The mechanism of religious conquest by absorption — Buddhist missionaries did not refute Shinto's gods, they reclassified them as local forms of Buddhas, producing Ryobu Shinto.

They discovered that whether Nature-gods or Man-gods they were nothing more than avatars or incarnations of the various Buddhas.

Why it matters

The mechanism of religious conquest by absorption — Buddhist missionaries did not refute Shinto's gods, they reclassified them as local forms of Buddhas, producing Ryobu Shinto.

Use when: When a new system wants to win without a fight, watch for reclassification — the old gods survive by becoming avatars of the new ones.

argument #213

Gods with no characters

Aston's claim that the ancient Japanese imagination produced hundreds of deities but almost no personalities — polytheism without portraiture, distinct from the Greek model.

They are very feebly characterised. Indeed, most of them may be said to have no characters at all.

Why it matters

Aston's claim that the ancient Japanese imagination produced hundreds of deities but almost no personalities — polytheism without portraiture, distinct from the Greek model.

Use when: When a pantheon feels thin, consider that weak characterization is the feature: gods as addressable powers, not characters.

argument #220

The offence was ritual, the Jewish moral

The sharpest one-line comparative ethics in the book — self-inflicted wounds, the cutting of dead bodies, and justifiable homicide all polluted equally, because pollution is about unsightliness before the gods, not wrongness.

It is the crime of taking away human life which is condemned in the Decalogue: the Ohoharahi objects to wounds as nasty, unsightly things, unmeet for a God to look upon or to be in any way associated with. In a word, the Japanese offence was ritual, the Jewish moral.

Why it matters

The sharpest one-line comparative ethics in the book — self-inflicted wounds, the cutting of dead bodies, and justifiable homicide all polluted equally, because pollution is about unsightliness before the gods, not wrongness.

Use when: When two rules look alike, compare the motive underneath — same prohibition, ritual motive versus moral motive, completely different religions.

argument #221

No ethics code as proof of goodness

The most audacious defense of a missing moral code in the book — the absence of ethics reframed as evidence of natural virtue rather than a gap.

Shintoists do not deny this feature of their religion, but claim that the absence of a code of ethics is a proof of the superior natural goodness of the Japanese nation. It needs no such artificial aids to virtuous conduct.

Why it matters

The most audacious defense of a missing moral code in the book — the absence of ethics reframed as evidence of natural virtue rather than a gap.

Use when: When someone defends the absence of a rule as proof of virtue, ask what happens when the virtue is tested — Aston spent the whole chapter showing the gap.

Concepts (1)

concept #218

Shrine means palace; festival means government

The sacred and the secular were never separated in ancient Japan — the language itself fuses worship and rule, with the Mikado as high priest and sovereign at once.

Miya meant equally shrine and palace. Matsuri, a Shinto festival, is the same word that we also find in Matsurigoto, government.

Why it matters

The sacred and the secular were never separated in ancient Japan — the language itself fuses worship and rule, with the Mikado as high priest and sovereign at once.

Use when: When analyzing a system where religion and state seem fused, look at the vocabulary — if one word does both jobs, the separation was never real.

contradiction #209

Shinto is not ancestor-worship

Corrects the most durable misunderstanding of Shinto — held even by most educated Japanese in Aston's day — by naming what the old gods actually were: Sun, Moon, Earth, Sea, Rain-storm, Fire, Thunder.

It is quite true that there is a large element of ancestor worship in modern Japanese religious practices, but a very little examination shows that all the great deities of the older Shinto are not Man, but Nature gods.

Why it matters

Corrects the most durable misunderstanding of Shinto — held even by most educated Japanese in Aston's day — by naming what the old gods actually were: Sun, Moon, Earth, Sea, Rain-storm, Fire, Thunder.

Use when: When a belief about a tradition comes mostly from its modern packaging, check the founding layer — the ancestors may be younger than the gods.

Definitions (5)

definition #210

A religion of love, not fear

Flips the default anthropology assumption — the saying 'Fear first made the Gods' does not hold here; the three greatest gods are beneficent beings and their festivals are occasions of rejoicing.

It is rather, as Schiller called the worship of the gods of Greece, a Wonnedienst, a religion inspired by love and gratitude more than by fear.

Why it matters

Flips the default anthropology assumption — the saying 'Fear first made the Gods' does not hold here; the three greatest gods are beneficent beings and their festivals are occasions of rejoicing.

Use when: When explaining why a religion endures, test the fear hypothesis against the love hypothesis — Shinto's staying power came from gratitude.

Not only human beings, but birds, beasts, plants and trees, seas and mountains, and all other things whatsoever which deserve to be dreaded and revered for the extraordinary and pre-eminent powers which they possess, are called Kami.

Shinto is doomed to extinction

Without a code of morals, or an efficient ecclesiastical organisation, with little aid from the arts of painting, sculpture, and architecture, and with a sacred literature scanty and feeble compared with those of its foreign rivals, Shinto is doomed to extinction.

Why it matters

Aston's 1921 closing verdict — delivered with total confidence, and worth keeping as a card precisely because history gets to grade it: the religion he buried is still alive.
