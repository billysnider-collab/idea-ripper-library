## Steal

"Here’s the operating principle: All other things being equal, you’ll be most persuasive to the people with whom you have the closest connection."

## Why it matters

Wikler ranks outreach by how close the relationship is. Talking to undecided friends and family sits at the top. Shouting at strangers online sits at the bottom. Campaigns call the first one "relational organizing" and treat it as cutting-edge, though it is old.

## Use when

You want to change minds and are about to start with the widest, least personal channel available.

## Steal

"Keep a CONSTANT WATCH on the DOUBTFUL VOTERS, and from time to time have them TALKED TO by those IN WHOM THEY HAVE THE MOST CONFIDENCE."

## Why it matters

Lincoln wrote this to a Whig campaign committee. Wikler's point is that today's relational-organizing apps add data on who might be doubtful and who has talked to whom. The underlying method is unchanged.

## Use when

You need to win over a wavering group and are deciding who should deliver the message.

## Steal

"Whenever a new way to reach someone unsolicitedly and cheaply pops into existence—mail, texts, Facebook messages—it has a brief twinkling moment of effectiveness as a form of direct voter contact. And then it gets so totally overused that voters largely tune it out."

## Why it matters

Low cost invites everyone in, and saturation kills the effect. Wikler ranks channels: face-to-face beats calls, calls beat postcards, and texts to strangers mostly annoy. Human connection is the one channel that doesn't fade.

## Use when

Your team is excited about a new cheap outreach channel because early numbers look great.

## Steal

"The most recent review of the available evidence found that a conversation with someone at their doorstep in a “high-salience” election like a presidential race results in a 3 percent boost in that person’s likelihood to vote. So, talk to thirty-three people and (on average) you’ll generate one vote."

## Why it matters

Wikler tells canvassers to set expectations low. About one door in five opens, and most conversations change nothing. Knowing the base rate keeps people going through dull hours, and shows that the effect is real.

## Use when

You're asking people to do slow, low-yield outreach and need them to understand what success looks like.

## Steal

"When someone on the fence says something that surprises you, don’t sputter. Don’t judge. Instead, remind yourself that you’ve just received a gift: a chance to talk to someone who, just maybe, could vote your way because you took the time to visit."

## Why it matters

Wikler starts every doorstep conversation by reminding himself he has no idea what is happening in the other person's life. The undecided person is the whole point of the visit. Reacting with judgment ends the one conversation that could have moved something.

## Use when

You're talking to someone on the fence and they say something that makes you want to argue.

## Steal

"When you see someone, flash them your winningest smile, and ask them if they can text three of their friends, right then, and remind them to vote early."

## Why it matters

Polling Place Vote Tripling, tested by the group Vote Rev, catches people at peak pride, just after voting. It turns strangers into relational organizers whose texts friends will actually read, and it reaches far more people per hour than knocking on doors.

## Use when

You have a stream of people who just did the thing you want and could ask their friends to do it too.

## Steal

"Perhaps the most notorious example of this phenomenon came in 2004, when the UK’s Guardian newspaper launched “Operation Clark County,” urging its global readership to send personal letters to voters in Clark County, Ohio, “citizen to citizen, explaining why this election matters to you.”"

## Why it matters

The letters produced an avalanche of rage from Ohio voters. Outsiders telling people how to vote reads as condescension. Wikler's rule: the more the contact rests on a real relationship, the bigger the impact.

## Use when

You're planning outreach to a community you don't belong to.

## Steal

"Every one of the tactics listed above, and the many others you’ll discover in this volume, will be more effective when organized by a team rather than just done by you yourself. Small groups are the secret sauce of politics."

## Why it matters

Individual effort is fragile and hard to sustain. A team spreads the work, keeps people coming back and builds the power needed to take on bigger opponents. Wikler's advice: join a group, or start one.

## Use when

You're trying to sustain a long effort on willpower alone.

## Steal

"The lower-profile the election, the bigger the impact of your work. If potential voters aren’t tuned in, volunteering makes a bigger difference."

## Why it matters

Swing voters in presidential years are buried in ads, mail and door hangers, so one more contact barely registers. In a city council race on a random Tuesday, one knock can decide a voter. Wikler calls this being an "elections hipster."

## Use when

You're choosing where to spend limited effort and the high-profile contest is already saturated.

## Steal

"I’m not asking you to do everything. I’m asking you to do something —regularly, for a long time."

"Movements, to flourish, need to give people time to fall back and recharge after their stints on the front lines."

## Why it matters

Wikler pairs this with Rabbi Tarfon's line that you are not obliged to finish the work but not free to neglect it. Rotation and modest, steady commitments keep volunteers around for years. Demanding everything burns them out in months.

## Use when

You're asking people to commit to a long cause and they look overwhelmed.

## Steal

"That helped inspire my own mantra in the 2022 election cycle: If you care about democracy, talk about roads."

## Why it matters

A De Pere city council candidate ran against an opponent tied to a national scandal. She campaigned on eviction help and a highway connector project, and won. Voters trust candidates who talk about their lives, and the abstract stakes come through by contrast.

## Use when

You care most about a big principle and your audience cares about their daily problems.

"For many people, all of politics—left, right, and center—is over there, whereas their real life is over here."

## Why it matters

In the survey Wikler cites, the biggest group of swing voters described their views in kitchen-table terms like "affordable" and "cost of living." They let the news come to them rather than seeking it out. A left-right pitch misses them entirely.

## Use when

You're pitching to people who don't follow your field and you're about to use your field's categories.

"The door knocking and voter contacting begins only after the team is launched."

## Why it matters

In the "snowflake" model behind the Obama campaigns, the paid organizer spends weeks in one-on-one coffees recruiting a core team, and each team recruits more volunteers. Wisconsin volunteers knocked on twice as many doors in 2018 as in 2016 at roughly half the cost.

## Use when

You're launching an effort and are tempted to start the work before you have people to lead it.

## Steal

"The party whose turnout drops by less from the presidential election is the one that wins the midterms."

## Why it matters

Turnout always falls in midterms. The winner is decided by which side's voters are more alarmed and mobilized. That is why the president's party nearly always loses seats.

## Use when

You're forecasting a contest where both sides will lose participation and the question is who loses less.
