## Steal

When you want a decision to land your way, frame the question so every option on the table still serves you — open war or covert guile, not surrender — leaving the room to argue over which of your paths to take.

## Why it matters

Satan opens the infernal council by declaring the goal fixed and offering only two paths: open war or covert guile. Both keep the war alive; the third path — submission, negotiation, peace — is silently removed from the menu. A debate over "which route" is already a concession that the destination is agreed. Whoever sets the menu has already won the destination.

## Use when

You run (or sit in) a meeting where the real options are missing and the 'debate' is between two routes that both feed the same agenda — or you want to notice someone doing this to you.

## Steal

Pre-empt competition for a role you hold by framing it as pure cost — 'who will envy the one who stands first against the enemy's aim?' — so challengers conclude there is nothing worth seizing.

## Why it matters

Satan has just been praised as leader, and his first concern is not strategy but competition: in Heaven the throne "might draw envy from each inferior." His defense is to redefine the throne as a liability — the one who sits highest stands foremost against the Thunderer's aim, exposed to the greatest share of pain. If the prize is a burden, only a fool reaches for it. Ambition is made to look irrational while he quietly keeps the decision rights that matter.

## Use when

You hold (or are watching someone hold) a position of power and want to disarm rivals — or you're noticing a leader describe their own job as all sacrifice to make ambition look foolish.

## Steal

When pushing an all-in gamble, reframe the status quo as already-maximal loss — 'what can be worse than this?' — so the downside disappears and even losing reads as a floor, not a loss.

## Why it matters

Moloch's whole argument rests on one premise: the current state is the worst conceivable, so nothing can be lost by gambling. He converts the fear of annihilation into permission — if God destroys them utterly, that is "happier far" than eternal misery — and caps it with a payoff that can't be audited: "which, if not victory, is yet revenge." Win or lose, the gamble "pays." When the downside is rhetorically deleted, the speaker is no longer comparing options. They're selling.

## Use when

Someone drives a team, company, or cause into an all-or-nothing move with 'we have nothing to lose' or 'we're already at rock bottom' — and you want to tell real downside from rhetorical despair.

## Steal

Recognize the sophist by the tell Milton states directly: a tongue that 'dropped manna' and can 'make the worse appear the better reason' — eloquence serving a predetermined low aim, not open reasoning.

## Why it matters

Belial is introduced as a beautiful speaker with ugly intent. His counsel is passivity dressed as prudence: war, open or concealed, he dissuades — do nothing, and hope God's anger "in time may much remit." The move is to make surrender sound like strategy: endure, adapt, "this horror will grow mild, this darkness light" — comfort projected onto a situation with no evidence. Milton's narrator refuses to be charmed, labeling the speech "false and hollow," "ignoble ease and peaceful sloth." The mechanism is named outright, so you can name it too.

## Use when

A smooth, plausible argument points toward inaction or a low aim, and you need to tell whether the polish is reasoning or dressing.

## Steal

Offer a third path — build your own, live to yourself, 'hard liberty before the easy yoke' — so the room that dreads both fighting and submitting has somewhere to land.

## Why it matters

Mammon rejects both war and return: war is hopeless, and even God's mercy would mean "forced hallelujahs" to one they hate. His alternative is independence — "our own good from ourselves" — flattered as "hard liberty before the easy yoke," calling submission "servile" and hardship "liberty." The crowd applauds, not because the plan is best, but because it is the least frightening of the three. Fear of the sword drives them to the comfortable option.

## Use when

A team is stuck between attacking a stronger rival and capitulating, and no one has proposed simply building their own thing — or you want to make that third path legible.

## Steal

Get your pre-decided outcome adopted by having a trusted second reject the other options and introduce your plan as a fresh third idea, so it reaches the room as discovery, not as your will.

## Why it matters

Beelzebub rises "with grave aspect," the picture of deliberation, dismisses both war and peace as traps, then produces the "new" idea: another world, "some new race, called Man" — attack God's newest creation instead of God himself. Then the narrator breaks the fourth wall: this counsel was "first devised by Satan, and in part proposed." The laundered decision: the real author stays silent, the trusted deputy presents it, and the room votes for it as if it had just been invented.

## Use when

A decision that was clearly already made gets presented as an open, brand-new idea by someone other than the decider — and you want to see whose plan it actually is.

## Steal

After the room commits to your plan, volunteer yourself and cut off all reply — 'this enterprise none shall partake with me' — so no rival wins cheap credit for the dangerous part you now own.

## Why it matters

The plan is settled; only the dangerous execution remains, and every fallen angel goes silent. Satan volunteers — then "prevented all reply," precisely so no one else can offer now and "win cheap the high repute which he through hazard huge must earn." Milton hands you the diagnostic himself: "close ambition varnished o'er with zeal." The self-sacrifice and the credit-hoard are the same gesture.

## Use when

A leader steps forward for the visible, risky task and instantly closes the floor — and you want to know whether it's sacrifice or credit-hoarding.

## Steal

Detect the moment pride becomes grievance: the claim that you lost only to force ('reason hath equalled, force hath made supreme') and that your resistance is therefore justice, not injury.

## Why it matters

Milton names the engine of the rebellion in one phrase: "high disdain from sense of injured merit." The logic runs: I am his equal in merit, therefore his power over me is illegitimate. A loss of status becomes a moral injury — not "I wanted to be on top" but "I was denied my rightful place." Pride, re-narrated as grievance, becomes self-righteousness, and the endgame is the line everyone quotes: "better to reign in Hell than serve in Heaven." Miserable sovereignty, preferred to subordinate dignity.

## Use when

You (or someone you're watching) nurse a grievance and you want to test whether it's genuinely about fairness or about injured pride wearing fairness's clothes.

## Steal

Treat inner state as portable: you carry the heaven or the hell into every place, so changing the address — job, city, relationship — won't change what you bring. Fix the mind, not the location.

## Why it matters

Cast into Hell, Satan asserts the one thing his conqueror cannot take: "the mind is its own place, and in itself can make a Heaven of Hell, a Hell of Heaven." The line is genuinely true — which is why Milton the ironist puts it in a liar's mouth. Satan uses a real truth to justify staying exactly as he is. The irony is the lesson's edge: the person most fluent in this truth is also the one refusing to apply it. He will not make a Heaven of Hell; he will only make a Hell of Heaven.

## Use when

You're deciding whether to change your circumstances (quit, move, leave) and want to know whether the problem will follow you.

## Steal

Spot the tell that someone would rather stay broken than lose face: the fix is known and available, and the only thing blocking it is 'what will they think of me.'

## Why it matters

In the soliloquy Satan comes within one word of repenting — "is there no place left for repentance?" — and answers himself: "none left but by submission." He has named the fix. What blocks it is not impossibility but image: "that word disdain forbids me, and my dread of shame among the spirits beneath." The cost of repair is the loss of face before the very crowd he misled. When the obstacle to repair is not capability but reputation, the person is negotiating with their image, not with the problem.

## Use when

Someone can articulate exactly what would fix their situation and still won't take it — and you want to see the real blocker.

## Steal

Catch fatalism doing self-license: 'ease would recant / vows made in pain' — predicting your own backslide as a reason never to start, instead of as a reason to build supports.

## Why it matters

Satan reasons that even if he repented, it wouldn't hold: "how soon would highth recall high thoughts." The clincher: "ease would recant vows made in pain, as violent and void" — promises made under pressure are null the moment the pressure lifts, so "never can true reconcilement grow." The mechanism: a prediction of failure is promoted to a certainty, and the certainty becomes the excuse for inaction. Fatalism is doing the work of a permission slip.

## Use when

You hear 'I've tried and failed before, I'll just fail again' — and you want to tell a prediction from a permission slip.

## Steal

Recognize the terminal move of a rationalization: when someone redefines the harmful thing as the good thing — 'evil, be thou my good' — they have stopped negotiating with their conscience and made the harm the goal.

## Why it matters

The soliloquy descends to a single irreversible sentence: "So farewell, hope; and with hope farewell, fear; farewell, remorse! All good to me is lost; evil, be thou my good." A triple farewell — hope, fear, remorse, the three things that could still pull him back — dismissed one by one. Then the inversion: rather than change course, he redefines the course as good. This is the end-state of every rationalization. Once harm is reframed as the aim, there is nothing left to negotiate with. The argument with the conscience is over, and the conscience lost.

## Use when

A person, team, or organization has stopped trying to course-correct and started arguing that the harm is actually the point — and you want to see where the line was crossed.

## Steal

Catch envy disguising itself as liberation-talk: 'knowledge forbidden / suspicious, reasonless' — the other's advantage reframed as a rule designed to keep you down, so tearing it down feels like justice.

## Why it matters

Satan watches Adam and Eve — "sight hateful, sight tormenting!" — and converts envy into an argument. The one rule, one forbidden tree, becomes "knowledge forbidden, suspicious, reasonless"; the master's rule is reframed as the master's insecurity, "invented with design to keep them low." This is the exact pitch he will later make to Eve. Dress envy as emancipation and the target will do the destruction for you.

## Use when

You feel a sudden flash of resentment at someone else's good fortune and want to check whether it's a real injustice or envy dressing up as principle.
