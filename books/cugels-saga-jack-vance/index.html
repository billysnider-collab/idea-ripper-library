Card #c1413Synthesis

Replay the predecessor's exploit: Cugel and Weamish's carrier

Before you design your own move, reverse-engineer the exploit your predecessor already ran.

— In our words · Source: Cugel's Saga (Jack Vance)

[Use when]

You inherit a process where the last operator was skimming: inspect the instruments (forms, scripts, access rights) before you interview anyone.

[Why it matters]

Cugel invents nothing: he inspects Weamish's wheeled carrier, finds the peg that releases its pivoting false top (real cases below, mud-filled decoys on top), and reruns the same theft on the next shipment. Systems confess through their tools faster than through their people.

Card #c1414Synthesis

The auditor as leverage: shall I put the question to Master Soldinck

Fix a padded bill by naming the third party who could inspect it, not by arguing the numbers.

— In our words · Source: Cugel's Saga (Jack Vance)

[Use when]

A vendor's invoice doesn't add up and you can mention the client or auditor who will eventually review those costs.

[Why it matters]

When Twango pads Cugel's account with illegible invoices, Cugel only wonders aloud, "from sheer idle curiosity, shall I put the question to Master Soldinck?" — and the "misplaced decimal" turns up at once. The threat of inspection does the work; the leverage is borrowed and never spent.

Card #c1415Synthesis

Security theater as misdirection: the wax-seal ceremony

Audit the gaps between checkpoints, because whoever designs the ceremony decides where everyone looks.

— In our words · Source: Cugel's Saga (Jack Vance)

[Use when]

Reviewing a custody chain (cash, inventory, release approvals): trace the asset through every unobserved transit segment, not just the signed checkpoints.

[Why it matters]

Cugel wraps the shipment in wax seals, strapped cases and a set procession, then lets the false-top carrier work in the one unwatched stretch of corridor. Visible controls attract attention; the loss happens in the handoffs between them.

Card #c1416Synthesis

Play both sides of the trade: Cugel as Soldinck and Fuscule

Where buyer and seller can't see each other, broker the gap and disclose your spread, or you are running Cugel's fraud.

— In our words · Source: Cugel's Saga (Jack Vance)

[Use when]

You can connect a supplier and a buyer who can't find each other: publish your margin before either side commits.

[Why it matters]

Cugel tells Fuscule "I am Master Soldinck" and tells Soldinck "I am Fuscule," pocketing the difference on the worm deal — which works only because each side verifies its own half and nobody checks the middle. Brokerage uses the same mechanics; disclosure is the whole difference.

Card #c1417Synthesis

Hide value in plain sight: the Spatterlight hat-clasp

Protect a valuable by displaying it as something ordinary instead of hiding it.

— In our words · Source: Cugel's Saga (Jack Vance)

[Use when]

Carrying one high-value item through a crowded, theft-prone place where a locked case would mark you as the target.

[Why it matters]

Cugel wires the Pectoral Sky-break Spatterlight to his hat, where it seems "only a hat-clasp": searchers hunt for hidden treasure, and every lockbox or false bottom tells them where to dig. An object in plain, mundane use gives them nothing to pattern-match.
